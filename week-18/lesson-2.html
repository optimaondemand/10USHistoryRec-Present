📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In January 1918, ten months before the war's end, Wilson presented Congress with a peace program in fourteen points, an address that became known as the Fourteen PointsFOR-teen poyntsWoodrow Wilson's January 1918 peace program calling for open diplomacy, self-determination, and a League of Nations.. It called for open diplomacy in place of secret treaties, freedom of the seas, and reduced armaments. It called for self-determination for the peoples of Europe's collapsing empires. His capstone fourteenth point proposed a "general association of nations." That association would guarantee political independence and help prevent future wars. It became the proposal for the League of Nationsleeg uv NAY-shunzAn international organization proposed by Wilson to prevent future wars, which the United States never joined.. Judged against any earlier peace settlement in European history, this was a genuinely idealistic vision. It aimed less at punishing the defeated than at removing the structural causes of war this course examined in Week 17's July Crisis.

The actual peace conference opened at Versailles, outside Paris, in January 1919. Four men, later called the Big Four, dominated its decisions: Wilson, France's Georges Clemenceauzhorzh kleh-mahn-SOHThe French premier at the Paris Peace Conference, skeptical of Wilson's idealism and determined to punish Germany., Britain's David Lloyd GeorgeDAY-vid loyd jorjThe British prime minister at the Paris Peace Conference, one of the "Big Four.", and Italy's Vittorio Orlandovit-TOR-ee-oh or-LAHN-dohThe Italian prime minister at the Paris Peace Conference, who walked out over Italian territorial claims to Fiume.. Clemenceau greeted Wilson's idealism with open skepticism. He reportedly quipped that God himself needed only Ten Commandments. Humanity still broke every one of them, Clemenceau added, so fourteen points seemed likely to fare no better. Orlando eventually walked out of the conference entirely. His dispute was over Italian territorial claims to the port city of Fiume, a reminder that even the victors could not fully agree among themselves.

The Treaty of VersaillesTREE-tee uv ver-SYThe 1919 treaty ending World War I, which imposed harsh terms on Germany, including reparations and a war-guilt clause. that emerged fell well short of Wilson's vision. France and Britain had borne the war's staggering costs directly on their own soil. They insisted on substantial German reparations. They also demanded a formal declaration of German war guilt, written into the treaty's Article 231, that Wilson's own principles had never called for. Colonial territories simply changed hands between the victors instead. Self-determination, the outcome Wilson's own language had implied for these territories, never actually arrived for them. The British economist John Maynard Keynesjahn MAY-nerd kaynzA British economist who resigned from the peace conference in protest and warned in The Economic Consequences of the Peace that harsh reparations would harm Germany's economy and future stability. attended the conference himself. He resigned from the British delegation in protest before it ended. His 1919 book, The Economic Consequences of the Peace, made a specific warning. Reparations this harsh, Keynes argued, would cripple Germany's economy and store up dangerous resentment for the future. Wilson accepted the compromises anyway. He kept his fourteenth point, the League of Nations, at the treaty's center. He believed the League itself could correct whatever else the treaty got wrong.

That bet failed at home before it could be tested abroad. The U.S. Senate split into three camps over ratification. Republicans led by Henry Cabot LodgeHEN-ree KAB-ut lahjThe Republican senator who led a bloc wanting reservations attached to the League of Nations treaty. wanted specific reservations protecting American sovereignty within the League. A smaller bloc of senators, nicknamed the Irreconcilablesir-ih-KAHN-sy-luh-bulzA Senate bloc, led by William Borah and Hiram Johnson, that opposed joining the League of Nations under any terms., opposed joining the League under any terms at all. William Borah and Hiram Johnson led that bloc. Wilson refused to work with either group. Instead, he toured the country by train that September. He gave dozens of speeches, trying to build public pressure for ratification without any reservations attached. The tour broke him physically. He collapsed after a speech in Pueblo, Colorado. Days later, on October 2, 1919, Wilson suffered a severe stroke. It left him partially paralyzed for the rest of his presidency. His wife, Edith WilsonEE-dith WIL-sunWoodrow Wilson's wife, who controlled access to him after his 1919 stroke and decided what reached his desk., controlled access to him for months afterward. She decided personally what reached his desk and what did not. A healthier Wilson might have accepted Lodge's reservations and secured ratification anyway. This one refused to compromise. The Senate rejected the treaty twice, once in November 1919 and again in March 1920, and the United States never joined the League of Nations it had done more than any other nation to create.

The same anxious mood that sank the League at home curdled, in that same year of 1919, into the nation's first Red Scarered skairA period of intense fear of communist and anarchist subversion in the United States in 1919-1920.. The wartime propaganda machine described in this week's first lesson had spent two years teaching Americans to fear foreign subversion. That fear pivoted easily from German spies to a new target: Bolshevik-inspired radicalism. A real, if small, current of American anarchists and socialists gave that fear some genuine substance. These were the descendants of Week 11's Haymarket-era movements, and a number of them did openly celebrate Russia's 1917 revolution. Attorney General A. His Palmer RaidsPAH-mer raydzA series of raids in 1919-1920 arresting and deporting thousands of suspected radicals with little regard for due process., carried out across late 1919 and early 1920, gave suspects minimal attention to due process. A young Justice Department official named J. Edgar Hoover compiled the case files behind many of those raids. It was an early step in a career that would shape federal law enforcement for decades. The same public fear colored the 1921 murder trial of Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo VanzettiSAH-koh and van-ZET-eeTwo Italian immigrant anarchists convicted of murder in 1921 on evidence historians consider troublingly thin, in a trial widely seen as shaped by anti-immigrant and anti-radical prejudice., two Italian immigrant anarchists. Historians still consider the evidence used to convict them troublingly thin. For many Americans, and for Italian immigrant communities especially, that case became a lasting symbol. It showed how easily the era treated foreign birth and radical politics as a form of guilt in themselves.

The semester that opened with Reconstruction's contested political settlement closes, fittingly, with another contested settlement, this one on a world stage. Its failure would shape the isolationism, and the anxious hunt for internal enemies, that mark the opening of the decade the next part of this course will take up.

Judged in Classical Republican terms, Wilson's peacemaking shows both real virtue and a real, costly vice. His vision of a world organized around law rather than raw power reflected a genuine moral seriousness, the same seriousness this course traced back to his religious formation in Week 16. But that same conviction hardened, at the crucial moment, into an unwillingness to accept Lodge's reservations even when compromise might have saved the League outright. Wilson's illness made that rigidity worse, but illness alone does not fully explain it; friends and rivals alike had long described Wilson as a man temperamentally unwilling to split the difference once his own mind was settled. A regime, and a person, can be judged partly by how well conviction bends to circumstance without breaking. On that measure, Wilson's peace fell short of his own war.

Across eighteen weeks, this course has followed a single political community working through the first-order problems every civic order must solve: how to bind citizens together after Reconstruction's rupture, how to organize productive economic life amid industrial upheaval, and how to defend itself and deliberate wisely once a war engulfed the wider world. None of it was inevitable. Specific men, making specific choices under real constraints, decided how each chapter actually turned out.
