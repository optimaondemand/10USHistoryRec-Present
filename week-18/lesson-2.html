📖 LESSON 18.3
Wilson's Fourteen Points and the Failed Peace
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In January 1918, ten months before the war's end, Wilson presented Congress with a Fourteen Points program for the peace to come: open diplomacy in place of secret treaties, freedom of the seas, reduced armaments, self-determination for the peoples of collapsing empires, and, as his capstone fourteenth point, a "general association of nations" to guarantee political independence and prevent future wars, the proposal that became the League of Nations. It was, by the standard of any peace settlement in European history to that point, a genuinely idealistic vision, aimed less at punishing the defeated than at removing the structural causes of war this course examined in Week 17's July Crisis.

The actual peace conference at Versailles in 1919 fell well short of that vision. France and Britain, having borne the war's staggering costs directly on their own soil, insisted on substantial German reparations and a formal declaration of German war guilt that Wilson's own principles had not called for, and colonial territories changed hands between the victors rather than moving toward self-determination as Wilson's language had implied they might. Wilson accepted these compromises in exchange for keeping his fourteenth point, the League of Nations, at the center of the treaty, believing the League itself could correct whatever else the treaty got wrong.

That bet failed at home before it could be tested abroad. The U.S. Senate, divided between Republicans led by Henry Cabot Lodge who wanted specific reservations protecting American sovereignty within the League and a smaller bloc opposed to the League altogether, twice declined to ratify the Treaty of Versailles, first in November 1919 and again in March 1920. Wilson, weakened by a stroke in October 1919 and unwilling to accept Lodge's proposed compromises, refused to negotiate, and the United States never joined the League of Nations it had done more than any other nation to create.

The same anxious mood that sank the League at home curdled, in that same year of 1919, into the first Red Scare: the wartime propaganda machine described in Monday's lesson, having spent two years teaching Americans to fear foreign subversion, pivoted easily from fearing German spies to fearing the same Bolshevik-inspired radicalism a small number of anarchists and socialists, the descendants of Week 11's Haymarket-era movements, genuinely did celebrate after Russia's 1917 revolution. Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer ordered thousands of arrests and deportations of suspected radicals with minimal due process in the Palmer Raids of late 1919 and early 1920, and the same public fear would color the murder trial of Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti, two Italian immigrant anarchists convicted in 1921 on evidence historians still consider troublingly thin, in a case that became, for many Americans and Italian immigrant communities alike, a symbol of the era's willingness to treat foreign birth and radical politics as themselves a form of guilt.

The semester that opened with Reconstruction's contested political settlement closes, fittingly, with another contested settlement, this one on a world stage, whose failure would shape the isolationism, and the anxious hunt for internal enemies, that mark the opening of the decade this course's Spring semester will take up.
