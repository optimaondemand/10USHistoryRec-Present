📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Two specific events, not a slow inevitable drift, brought the United States from the neutrality of Week 17 into the war. In January 1917, British codebreakers in a secret unit called Room 40 intercepted and decoded a telegram from Germany's foreign secretary, Arthur Zimmermann, a message that became known as the Zimmermann TelegramTSIM-er-mun TEL-uh-gramA secret German proposal, intercepted by British codebreakers in January 1917, offering Mexico Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona in exchange for attacking the United States.. It proposed a secret alliance with Mexico. Germany promised Mexico the return of Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. Mexico would only need to attack the United States, and only if America entered the war. When the telegram became public, some Americans suspected a British forgery, designed to drag the country into war. Zimmermann himself removed that doubt. He publicly admitted the telegram was genuine at a press conference in Berlin.

Weeks later, Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfareun-rih-STRIK-ted SUB-muh-reen WOR-fairGermany's 1917 policy of sinking any ship, including neutral vessels, without warning. against all shipping in British waters, including neutral American vessels. Germany had suspended that same policy after the Lusitania crisis, covered in Week 16, precisely to avoid provoking American entry. German naval planners now made a calculated gamble instead. They believed unrestricted submarine warfare could starve Britain into surrender within about five months. American troops, they calculated, could not reach Europe in any real numbers before that surrender came. It was a bet on speed, and it turned out to be wrong. Faced with a direct threat to Mexico and a direct provocation at sea within weeks of each other, Wilson acted. He asked Congress for a declaration of war on April 2, 1917, arguing "the world must be made safe for democracy." Congress obliged four days later.

Turning a nation of roughly four million active and reserve soldiers, mostly untrained, into a force capable of fighting in France required an entirely new mobilizationmoh-bih-lih-ZAY-shunThe process of organizing and deploying a country's armed forces and supplies for war. apparatus. The Selective Service Act of May 1917sih-LEK-tiv SER-viss akt uv NYN-teen SEV-en-teenThe law authorizing the first American military draft since the Civil War. authorized the first American conscription since the Civil War. It eventually registered some twenty-four million men. Secretary of War Newton Baker drew the first draft numbers that July. He was blindfolded for the occasion, reaching into a large glass bowl in Washington. Financier Bernard Baruch chaired the new War Industries Boardwor IN-dus-treez bordA federal agency, chaired by Bernard Baruch, that coordinated wartime industrial production.. His agency ran factories nationwide, putting war production ahead of civilian goods. Herbert Hoover, already known for organizing Belgian famine relief earlier in the war, headed a new Food Administrationfood ad-min-ih-STRAY-shunA federal agency, headed by Herbert Hoover, that encouraged voluntary food conservation during World War I.. Hoover's agency asked Americans to observe voluntary "Meatless Mondays" and "Wheatless Wednesdays," freeing up food supplies for troops and allies overseas rather than imposing mandatory rationing by law. Liberty BondsLIB-er-tee bahndzWar bonds sold to ordinary citizens to help finance the American war effort. financed much of the war's actual cost, sold to ordinary citizens through four separate nationwide drives. Movie stars including Mary Pickford, Charlie Chaplin, and Douglas Fairbanks toured the country to help sell them.

Journalist George Creel headed a new government propaganda agency, the Committee on Public Informationkuh-MIT-ee ahn PUB-lik in-for-MAY-shunA government propaganda agency, led by George Creel, that built public support for World War I.. Its job was building enthusiastic public support for a war many Americans had opposed just months earlier. Creel recruited some seventy-five thousand volunteer speakers, nicknamed the Four Minute Men. They delivered short patriotic talks in movie theaters, timed to fit between reel changes. Creel also enlisted commercial illustrators through a Division of Pictorial Publicity. The well-known artist Charles Dana Gibson chaired that division, producing recruitment and bond posters by the millions. That same wartime enthusiasm, deliberately manufactured in large part by Creel's own agency, had a darker edge. It sometimes shaded into outright suppression of dissent, written into law in the Espionage and Sedition ActsES-pee-uh-nahzh and sih-DISH-un aktsWartime laws restricting speech and dissent against the war effort., laws this course examines further in a later unit. A private volunteer group called the American Protective Leagueuh-MAIR-ih-kun proh-TEK-tiv leegA private volunteer group that investigated neighbors and coworkers for suspected disloyalty during World War I. grew alongside this official campaign. Its roughly two hundred fifty thousand members, working with limited government oversight, investigated neighbors and coworkers for suspected disloyalty. Sauerkraut was briefly renamed "liberty cabbage" on some restaurant menus. German-language instruction disappeared from many American schools almost overnight. The same patriotic energy that filled draft quotas and sold Liberty Bonds could tip quickly into suspicion of anyone who looked, sounded, or sympathized with the enemy.

By late 1917 and through 1918, roughly two million American soldiers deployed to France. Pershingjahn jay PER-shingThe general who commanded the American Expeditionary Forces and insisted American troops fight as a separate army rather than being scattered into French and British units.. French and British commanders pressured Pershing hard to scatter these fresh American troops directly into their own depleted units. Pershing refused. He insisted American troops fight together, as their own separate army under American command. A unified American force, however green, would fight better, he judged. It would also matter more at the eventual peace table than scattered American reinforcements ever could. Allied commanders eventually accepted his terms. Pershing did lend individual American divisions temporarily, though, during Germany's last desperate offensive in the spring of 1918. American troops helped hold that offensive at Belleau Wood in June. They then anchored the Meuse-Argonne Offensivemyooz ar-GAHN uh-FEN-sivThe largest battle in American military history to that point, fought in the fall of 1918. that September and October, the largest battle in American military history up to that point. The combined weight of a battered but intact Allied line, joined by fresh American numbers, finally forced Germany to seek an armistice. It was signed at the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month, November 11, 1918.

American losses, roughly 116,000 dead, were far smaller than the millions lost by the war's other major combatants. But the shock of even that scale of loss struck hard. It arrived concentrated into barely a year and a half of active combat. That shock transformed a country that had watched the war from a distance for nearly three years.

Judged against Week 17's Preparedness debate, the mobilization described here answers Roosevelt's old warning more than Wilson's old caution. Once war actually arrived, the country built, in barely eighteen months, the conscription system, industrial coordination, and finance apparatus Roosevelt had wanted years earlier. That speed is itself a real achievement in Classical Republican terms. A political community showed, under real pressure, the capacity to organize and defend itself. Hegel treated exactly that capacity as the ultimate test of a state's health. The same mobilization also carried real costs at home. The Espionage Act chilled honest dissent, and the American Protective League's volunteers spied informally on ordinary citizens. This course does not need to minimize those costs simply because the war effort itself, in the narrow military sense, ultimately succeeded.
