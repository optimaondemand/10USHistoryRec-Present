For a few years, federal troops, federal marshals, and this new federal legal power made Black political participation in the South a genuine, if fragile, reality. By the early 1870s, more than a dozen Black congressmen and hundreds of Black state legislators had been elected across the former Confederacy. The federal government seemed stuck fighting in the South while also drowning in its own corruption.

The Panic of 1873PAN-ik uv AY-teen SEV-en-tee-threeA severe economic depression triggered by the collapse of the banking house Jay Cooke and Company, which pushed voters to lose patience with continued federal enforcement in the South. began in September 1873, when the banking house of Jay Cookejay kookThe banker whose company's 1873 collapse triggered the Panic of 1873. and Company collapsed. It had made too many loans to overbuilt railroads, including lines racing to lay track ahead of any real demand for it. The panic that followed threw thousands of businesses into bankruptcy. It left millions of Americans out of work for years. That economic depression pushed voters to care more about jobs than about federal troops stationed in South Carolina. Northern Republicans who had supported ReconstructionREK-un-STRUK-shunThe twelve-year period after the Civil War (1865-1877) when the U.S. government worked to readmit the former Confederate states and define citizenship for freedpeople. on principle began to ask a hard question: how much longer should the North's soldiers and patience be spent on the South's internal politics? By Grant's second term, Southern Democrats calling themselves "Redeemersrih-DEE-merzSouthern Democrats who used ballots and violence to retake state governments from Reconstruction-era Republican control in the 1870s." were already retaking state governments one by one. Sometimes they won by ballot, and sometimes by the same paramilitary violence the Enforcement Acts had been written to stop. In 1875, Mississippi's Redeemers used a campaign of organized violence and intimidation that contemporaries called the Mississippi Planmih-sih-SIH-pee planAn 1875 campaign of organized violence and intimidation used by Mississippi Redeemers to seize control of the state government. to reclaim the state. Grant declined the governor's request for federal troops that year. He worried that Northern voters, already weary of repeated Southern crises, would punish his own party at the polls that fall. By 1876, only three Southern states, South Carolina, Louisiana, and Florida, still had Reconstruction governments backed by federal troops.

Those three states became the center of the most contested presidential election in American history. In November 1876, Democrat Samuel TildenSAM-yoo-el TIL-denThe Democratic candidate who won the popular vote in the disputed 1876 presidential election. of New York won the national popular vote. He appeared to have won the electoral count as well, needing only one more electoral vote to reach the majority required. But South Carolina, Louisiana, and Florida each sent Congress two competing sets of election returns. One certified Republican Rutherford B. HayesRUTH-er-ford bee hayzThe Republican candidate declared the winner of the disputed 1876 election by the Electoral Commission. Withdrew federal troops from the South as part of the Compromise of 1877., and the other certified Tilden, and neither party would accept the other's count. In each of the three disputed states, both parties claimed victory amid widespread fraud. In South Carolina and Louisiana especially, armed threats against Black voters on election day made an honest count nearly impossible. No constitutional rule clearly covered the dispute, so Congress created a fifteen-member Electoral Commissionih-LEK-tor-ul kuh-MISH-unA fifteen-member body of representatives, senators, and Supreme Court justices created by Congress in 1877 to resolve the disputed presidential election, ruling 8-7 for Hayes along party lines. to decide it. The commission was made up of five representatives, five senators, and five Supreme Court justices, evenly split between the parties except for one supposedly independent justice, David Davis. Davis resigned from the Court to take a Senate seat just before the commission began its work, and his replacement was a Republican, Joseph Bradley. The commission then ruled 8 to 7, along party lines, in favor of Hayes on every disputed state.

Even an 8 to 7 ruling could not settle the matter by itself. House Democrats could still delay counting the votes past Inauguration Day. In a series of private meetings, remembered as the Compromise of 1877KOM-prih-myz uv AY-teen SEV-en-tee-SEV-enThe informal bargain in which Hayes took the presidency in exchange for withdrawing the last federal troops protecting Reconstruction governments in the South, ending Reconstruction., allies of Hayes and Southern Democrats reached an informal bargain. Democrats would let the electoral count finish and Hayes would take office. In return, Hayes would withdraw the last federal troops protecting Reconstruction governments in South Carolina and Louisiana. Hayes kept his side of the bargain within weeks of his inauguration in March 1877. Without federal troops to support them, the remaining Reconstruction governments collapsed almost at once. Redeemer Democrats took full control of every former Confederate state for the first time since the war. Freed from federal oversight, the new state legislatures moved quickly to write vagrancy lawsVAY-grun-see lawzLaws criminalizing unemployment, used after 1877 to arrest and control Black Southerners., convict-leasingKON-vikt LEE-singA system in which states leased prisoners, disproportionately Black men convicted under vagrancy laws, to private companies for forced labor. systems, and voting restrictions that echoed the Black Codesblak kohdzLaws passed by Southern state legislatures after the war that restricted freedpeople's rights to work, own land, and testify in court, in practice recreating many conditions of slavery. from the previous lesson. This time, no Enforcement Acts were left to stop them. The fuller story of what this "Redemption" meant for Black Southerners, sharecropping, debt peonage, and the legal system that would become known as Jim Crow, is the subject of Week 8.

None of what happened in 1877 was required by the Constitution or by the promises of the 14th AmendmentFOR-teenth uh-MEND-mentThe 1868 constitutional amendment guaranteeing citizenship and equal protection under the law to everyone born in the United States.. A different ruling from the Electoral Commission could have kept Reconstruction alive well past 1877. So could a different bargain in those private meetings, or a Congress with more patience left for Southern enforcement. That it ended instead was, like the settlement traced in the previous lesson, the product of specific choices made by specific men in a specific crisis. It was not the working-out of any historical necessity.
