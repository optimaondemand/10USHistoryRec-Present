This Week's Topic
Reconstruction: Political Settlement and New Constitutions in the South
📖 LESSON 1.3
The Grant Presidency
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Ulysses S. Grant won the presidency in 1868 as the general who had brought the Confederacy to surrender. He brought a soldier's directness to the office. The Ku Klux Klan rose up across the South to terrorize Black voters and the white Republicans who supported them. Grant pushed the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871 through Congress in response. These laws made interference with voting rights a federal crime. They also let Grant suspend habeas corpus in parts of South Carolina to break the Klan's power there. Congress created the Department of Justice in 1870 for a related reason: it gave the federal government the legal muscle to prosecute Klan violence across state lines. Its first attorney general, Amos Akerman, pursued hundreds of Klan prosecutions in South Carolina alone. Akerman's zeal made him enemies within Grant's own cabinet and among railroad interests who found him too aggressive. Grant eased him out of office by the end of 1871, a first sign that even Grant's own commitment to enforcement had limits. For a few years, federal troops, federal marshals, and this new federal legal power made Black political participation in the South a genuine, if fragile, reality. By the early 1870s, more than a dozen Black congressmen and hundreds of Black state legislators had been elected across the former Confederacy. Joseph Rainey of South Carolina became the first Black member of the House of Representatives in 1870. Blanche K. Bruce of Mississippi joined Hiram Revels as the second Black U.S. senator in 1875.

Grant's own reputation for personal honesty was not in question. But he trusted his own men freely and looked into their conduct only rarely. His presidency became a byword for scandal. In the Crédit Mobilier affair, railroad insiders bribed congressmen with discounted stock, part of the same scheme that funded the transcontinental railroad discussed in Week 3. In the Whiskey Ring, distillers and Treasury officials conspired to skim millions of dollars in tax revenue. That scheme reached Grant's own private secretary, Orville Babcock, though Grant refused to believe his friend was guilty. A third scandal involved bribes at Indian trading posts. It reached all the way to Grant's secretary of war, William Belknap, who resigned minutes before the House could impeach him. None of these scandals touched Grant's own integrity directly. But each one gave Northern voters one more reason to grow tired of Washington. The federal government seemed stuck fighting in the South while also drowning in its own corruption.

The Panic began in September 1873, when the banking house of Jay Cooke and Company collapsed. It had made too many loans to overbuilt railroads, including lines racing to lay track ahead of any real demand for it. The panic that followed threw thousands of businesses into bankruptcy. It left millions of Americans out of work for years. That economic depression pushed voters to care more about jobs than about federal troops stationed in South Carolina. Northern Republicans who had supported Reconstruction on principle began to ask a hard question: how much longer should the North's soldiers and patience be spent on the South's internal politics? By Grant's second term, Southern Democrats calling themselves "Redeemers" were already retaking state governments one by one. Sometimes they won by ballot, and sometimes by the same paramilitary violence the Enforcement Acts had been written to stop. In 1875, Mississippi's Redeemers used a campaign of organized violence and intimidation that contemporaries called the Mississippi Plan to reclaim the state. Grant declined the governor's request for federal troops that year. He worried that Northern voters, already weary of repeated Southern crises, would punish his own party at the polls that fall. By 1876, only three Southern states, South Carolina, Louisiana, and Florida, still had Reconstruction governments backed by federal troops.

Those three states became the center of the most contested presidential election in American history. In November 1876, Democrat Samuel Tilden of New York won the national popular vote. He appeared to have won the electoral count as well, needing only one more electoral vote to reach the majority required. But South Carolina, Louisiana, and Florida each sent Congress two competing sets of election returns. One certified Republican Rutherford B. Hayes, and the other certified Tilden, and neither party would accept the other's count. In each of the three disputed states, both parties claimed victory amid widespread fraud. In South Carolina and Louisiana especially, armed threats against Black voters on election day made an honest count nearly impossible. No constitutional rule clearly covered the dispute, so Congress created a fifteen-member Electoral Commission to decide it. The commission was made up of five representatives, five senators, and five Supreme Court justices, evenly split between the parties except for one supposedly independent justice, David Davis. Davis resigned from the Court to take a Senate seat just before the commission began its work, and his replacement was a Republican, Joseph Bradley. The commission then ruled 8 to 7, along party lines, in favor of Hayes on every disputed state.

Even an 8 to 7 ruling could not settle the matter by itself. House Democrats could still delay counting the votes past Inauguration Day. In a series of private meetings, remembered as the Compromise of 1877, allies of Hayes and Southern Democrats reached an informal bargain. Democrats would let the electoral count finish and Hayes would take office. In return, Hayes would withdraw the last federal troops protecting Reconstruction governments in South Carolina and Louisiana. Hayes kept his side of the bargain within weeks of his inauguration in March 1877. Without federal troops to support them, the remaining Reconstruction governments collapsed almost at once. Redeemer Democrats took full control of every former Confederate state for the first time since the war. Freed from federal oversight, the new state legislatures moved quickly to write vagrancy laws, convict-leasing systems, and voting restrictions that echoed the Black Codes from the previous lesson. This time, no Enforcement Acts were left to stop them. The fuller story of what this "Redemption" meant for Black Southerners, sharecropping, debt peonage, and the legal system that would become known as Jim Crow, is the subject of Week 8.

None of what happened in 1877 was required by the Constitution or by the promises of the 14th Amendment. A different ruling from the Electoral Commission could have kept Reconstruction alive well past 1877. So could a different bargain in those private meetings, or a Congress with more patience left for Southern enforcement. That it ended instead was, like the settlement traced in the previous lesson, the product of specific choices made by specific men in a specific crisis. It was not the working-out of any historical necessity.
