📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On the night of April 14, 1865, an actor fired a single bullet. That shot reshaped the next twelve years of American history more than any law Congress would pass. Abraham Lincoln had spent the war's final weeks sketching a lenient peace. He wanted swift readmission for the seceded states and only light punishment for their people. In his own words, he asked for "malice toward none, charity for all." Even before his death, Lincoln and Congress had already clashed over how lenient that peace should be. Had he lived out his second term, the two branches might have kept fighting over this question. Or Lincoln's political skill might have found a compromise. Instead the office fell to Vice President Andrew JohnsonAN-droo JAHN-sunThe Tennessee Democrat and vice president who became president after Lincoln's assassination and pursued a lenient Reconstruction policy toward the South.. Johnson was a Tennessee Democrat who had stayed loyal to the Union. But he shared little of Lincoln's goodwill toward the freedpeopleFREED-pee-pulFormerly enslaved Black Americans freed by the Civil War and the 13th Amendment., whose future now hung on decisions made in Washington.

Johnson pressed ahead with a plan nearly as lenient as Lincoln's. He pardoned most Confederates and restored state governments quickly. He left the treatment of Black Southerners to the states themselves. The Southern legislatures elected under this plan answered with the Black Codesblak kohdzLaws passed by Southern state legislatures after the war that restricted freedpeople's rights to work, own land, and testify in court, in practice recreating many conditions of slavery.. These laws criminalized unemployment for freedpeople. A sheriff could arrest anyone who could not prove they had a job. The laws restricted Black Americans' right to own farmland outside of towns. They barred Black witnesses from testifying against white citizens in court. Many freedpeople were bound to year-long labor contracts that looked, in practice, very much like the slavery the war had just abolished. In Mississippi and South Carolina, the laws went furthest, controlling where freedpeople could live and work down to the smallest detail. To the Republicans who controlled Congress, this looked like the Confederacy trying to win on paper what it had lost on the battlefield.

Freedpeople did not simply accept the Black Codes without a response. Many organized their own conventions across the South in 1865 and 1866. None of this was inevitable. A surviving Lincoln, a more accommodating Congress, or a different set of choices by Southern legislatures in 1865 and 1866 could each have produced a milder, faster reunion. Any of those paths would have meant far less direct federal supervision of the South. That it went the way it did was the product of specific decisions, made by specific men, in a narrow window of years. Those decisions bought the freedpeople of the South a decade of real, if contested, political power. Whether the North had the will to defend that power for longer than a decade, and what happened once that will ran out, is the question the next lesson takes up.

PEOPLES: Freedpeople at the Ballot Box

This 1867 engraving from Harper's Weekly shows Black Virginians casting ballots for the first time, made possible by the Reconstruction Act of 1867 this lesson describes. The artist, Alfred Waud, deliberately drew a laborer, a businessman, and a soldier standing together in line, a statement that the vote crossed lines of trade and class among freedpeople. More than ninety thousand Black Virginians registered and voted in the October 1867 election pictured here, choosing delegates to write the state's new constitution.

Scenes like this one repeated across the former Confederacy in the years just after this lesson's events. The political power they represent was steadily rolled back after 1877, once federal troops withdrew, the subject of the next lesson.
