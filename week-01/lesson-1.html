📖 LESSON 1.1
The Political Settlement of Reconstruction
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On the night of April 14, 1865, an actor fired a single bullet. That shot reshaped the next twelve years of American history more than any law Congress would pass. Abraham Lincoln had spent the war's final weeks sketching a lenient peace. He wanted swift readmission for the seceded states and only light punishment for their people. In his own words, he asked for "malice toward none, charity for all." Even before his death, Lincoln and Congress had already clashed over how lenient that peace should be. In 1864, Congress passed the Wade-Davis Bill. It demanded that a majority of a state's white male citizens, not just ten percent, swear loyalty before that state could rejoin the Union. Lincoln killed the bill with a pocket veto. He wanted to keep control of Reconstruction in his own hands. Had he lived out his second term, the two branches might have kept fighting over this question. Or Lincoln's political skill might have found a compromise. Instead the office fell to Vice President Andrew Johnson. Johnson was a Tennessee Democrat who had stayed loyal to the Union. But he shared little of Lincoln's goodwill toward the freedpeople, whose future now hung on decisions made in Washington.

Johnson pressed ahead with a plan nearly as lenient as Lincoln's. He pardoned most Confederates and restored state governments quickly. He left the treatment of Black Southerners to the states themselves. The Southern legislatures elected under this plan answered with the Black Codes. These laws criminalized unemployment for freedpeople. A sheriff could arrest anyone who could not prove they had a job. The laws restricted Black Americans' right to own farmland outside of towns. They barred Black witnesses from testifying against white citizens in court. Many freedpeople were bound to year-long labor contracts that looked, in practice, very much like the slavery the war had just abolished. In Mississippi and South Carolina, the laws went furthest, controlling where freedpeople could live and work down to the smallest detail. To the Republicans who controlled Congress, this looked like the Confederacy trying to win on paper what it had lost on the battlefield.

Freedpeople did not simply accept the Black Codes without a response. Many organized their own conventions across the South in 1865 and 1866. There they demanded full citizenship and the right to testify in court. Some also demanded a share of the land they had worked as enslaved people. General William T. Sherman's Special Field Order No. 15, issued in January 1865, had briefly set aside coastal land in Georgia and South Carolina for freed families. That order is the origin of the phrase "forty acres and a mule." President Johnson revoked the order within the year and returned the land to its former owners. This ended the freedpeople's best chance at real economic independence before it properly began. The Freedmen's Bureau, a federal agency created in March 1865, tried to fill part of the gap. It built and staffed schools, negotiated fairer labor contracts, and gave out emergency food and medical care across the defeated South. Its work was real, but its funding and staff were always thin next to the size of the task. Independent Black churches, especially the African Methodist Episcopal Church, spread rapidly across the South in these same years. They gave freedpeople institutions of their own that no white legislature could regulate away.

What followed was one of the sharpest constitutional fights between a president and Congress in American history. A faction known as the Radical Republicans, led by Thaddeus Stevens in the House and Charles Sumner in the Senate, argued that the defeated South had forfeited any claim to prompt readmission. They insisted that Congress, not the president, held the constitutional authority to set the terms of reunion. Johnson vetoed the Civil Rights Act of 1866. That act declared that all persons born in the United States, regardless of race, were citizens. It entitled them to make contracts, own property, and sue in court on equal terms with white citizens, rights the Black Codes had just denied. Johnson also vetoed the renewal of the Freedmen's Bureau. Congress overrode him on both bills, something that had almost never happened to an American president before. Congress then passed the Tenure of Office Act in 1867. It barred the president from removing certain officials, including cabinet members, without Senate approval. Johnson tested the law directly in 1868 by firing his secretary of war, Edwin Stanton, a Radical ally Johnson distrusted. The House impeached Johnson within days. His Senate trial ended in a vote of 35 guilty to 19 not guilty, exactly one vote short of the two-thirds needed for conviction. That single vote belonged to Republican senator Edmund Ross of Kansas. His choice to acquit Johnson cost him his political career, but it kept the presidency intact.

Congress's larger answer took constitutional form in the 14th Amendment, ratified in 1868. It guaranteed citizenship and equal protection under the law to everyone born in the United States, a promise no state could take back. It also reduced a state's representation in Congress if that state denied the vote to its male citizens. And it barred former Confederate officials from holding office unless Congress specifically pardoned them. Several Southern states refused to ratify the amendment. In response, Congress passed the Reconstruction Act of 1867 over Johnson's veto. This law divided the former Confederacy into five military districts. It required each state to write a new constitution guaranteeing Black male suffrage before it could send representatives back to Washington. By 1868, new state governments across the South had begun the work of rebuilding under these rules. For the first time in American history, they included Black officeholders. Hiram Revels of Mississippi became the first Black member of the United States Senate in 1870. Two years later, P.B.S. Pinchback briefly served as governor of Louisiana, the first Black American to hold that office anywhere in the country.

None of this was inevitable. A surviving Lincoln, a more accommodating Congress, or a different set of choices by Southern legislatures in 1865 and 1866 could each have produced a milder, faster reunion. Any of those paths would have meant far less direct federal supervision of the South. That it went the way it did was the product of specific decisions, made by specific men, in a narrow window of years. Those decisions bought the freedpeople of the South a decade of real, if contested, political power. Whether the North had the will to defend that power for longer than a decade, and what happened once that will ran out, is the question the next lesson takes up.
