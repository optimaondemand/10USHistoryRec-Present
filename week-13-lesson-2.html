📖 LESSON 13.3
JP Morgan and Northern Securities
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In 1901, financier J.P. Morgan and railroad magnates James J. Hill and E.H. Harriman fought for control of the Northern Pacific and Great Northern railroads so fiercely that competing stock purchases briefly triggered a Wall Street panic. To end the fight and secure their combined control, the rivals agreed to fold both railroads into a single holding company, the Northern Securities Company, a solution that made perfect sense to the financiers involved and eliminated, at a stroke, all competition between two of the most important rail lines in the northern United States.

Roosevelt's Justice Department sued to dissolve Northern Securities under the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1902, a genuinely bold move against the most powerful banker in America, at a time when many assumed the Sherman Act applied only to blatant price-fixing schemes like the trusts of Week 4, not to a legal merger of stock ownership. Morgan reportedly told Roosevelt he should simply "send your man to my man" to work out any problem privately, missing entirely that Roosevelt intended to settle the matter in court, as a matter of public law, not private negotiation between powerful men.

The Supreme Court's 1904 decision, by a narrow five-to-four vote, upheld the government's case: even a merger that took the legal form of common stock ownership, rather than an explicit price-fixing agreement, could violate the Sherman Act if its practical effect was to destroy competition between companies that would otherwise compete. It was Roosevelt's first great trust-busting victory as president, and it established a legal principle, that substance rather than legal form determines whether a combination illegally restrains trade, that antitrust law has relied on ever since.
