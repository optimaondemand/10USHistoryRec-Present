📖 LESSON 6.2
Thomas Edison and the Light Bulb
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Other inventors had produced electric light before Edison. Arc lighting, which draws a blazing spark between two carbon rods, already lit some city streets by the 1870s. It was far too harsh and too dangerous for a parlor. In England, Joseph Swan was working on an incandescent lamp along lines close to Edison's own. The two men's British interests were eventually merged into a single company rather than fought over in court. Edison's achievement in 1879 was therefore not the idea of electric light. It was a stubbornly practical problem. He needed a filament that would glow inside a vacuum bulb for hours without burning out. It also had to be cheap enough to replace gas lighting in an ordinary home.

The answer required patience of an unusual kind. Edison and his team tested material after material. He later claimed to have constructed three thousand different theories about the electric light. Experiment confirmed only two of them. In October 1879 a carbonized cotton thread burned for roughly fourteen hours, which was a genuine breakthrough by the standards of the day. Edison then sent men to South America and Asia hunting better fibers, and a carbonized bamboo filament served his commercial lamps for years afterward. Behind the trial and error, though, sat one piece of hard reasoning that mattered more than any single material. Edison worked out that a filament with high electrical resistance would draw very little current. Low current meant he could distribute power through thin copper wire rather than through the enormously expensive thick cable his rivals would need. The insight was economic as much as scientific, and it is why his system could be sold.

What made Edison historically significant was not only his patience with failure, real as that patience was, but the organization he built around it. Menlo Park, New Jersey, was not a lone tinkerer's workshop. Edison called it an invention factory, and the description was exact. He hired a salaried team, including the mathematician Francis Upton and the machinist Charles Batchelor. They worked through a research problem the way a mill works through raw material. Failures were recorded rather than hidden, because a failed material was information the next trial could use. This was itself a new kind of institution. It industrialized invention the way railroads had industrialized transport and Standard Oil had industrialized refining, and every corporate research laboratory since descends from it.

A working bulb was useless without a system to power it, and Edison understood this from the beginning. He designed the generators, the underground wiring, the sockets, and the fuses. He designed the meters that would let a company bill a customer for what he actually used. He also priced the whole thing deliberately against gas, because gas was the competitor a householder would compare it to. The Pearl Street Station opened in lower Manhattan in September 1882. It was the first commercial power plant in the United States, and it lit several hundred nearby customers with direct current. Edison's perseverance, the classical virtue this course associates with sustained effort toward a genuine common good, is best measured not by the bulb alone. It is measured by his willingness to solve every piece of the problem that made the bulb worth having.

That same determination showed a harder edge a few years later. Direct current, the system Edison had built, loses power badly over distance and could serve only a small radius from each station. Alternating current can be stepped up to high voltage for long-distance transmission, then stepped back down at the other end. George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla understood this and pursued it. Edison had an enormous investment in direct current. He fought back with a public campaign designed to convince Americans that alternating current was lethal. His associates staged public electrocutions of animals. Edison's laboratory also helped develop the electric chair using alternating current, so that the method of execution would carry his rival's name. He lost anyway. Westinghouse lit the Chicago world's fair of 1893 with alternating current, and a hydroelectric plant at Niagara Falls sent power to Buffalo two years later. Judged in the terms this course uses, the episode shows a settled trait working against the common good. Edison was defending an investment rather than pursuing a truth, and he knew the difference.

Edison was also far from the only inventor of this era, and several others deserve naming here. Their work is less famous only because of who they were, not because of what they achieved. Granville T. Woods, a Black inventor whom contemporaries sometimes called the Black Edison, patented dozens of devices. The most important was an induction telegraph. It let moving trains communicate with stations and with each other, a genuine safety advance that reduced collisions on crowded track. Edison twice challenged Woods's claim to that invention in court, and twice lost. He then offered Woods a position at his own company, and Woods declined it. Margaret Knight, working in the same decades, designed a machine that automatically folded and glued flat-bottomed paper bags. The design is still recognizable in grocery bags today. A man named Charles Annan copied her machine and filed for the patent himself. In court he argued that a woman could not possibly have understood the mechanics involved. Knight defeated him in 1871 by producing her own drawings, notes, and the iron model she had built.

A third name belongs beside them, and this one was inside Edison's own operation. Lewis Howard Latimer was the son of formerly enslaved parents, and he taught himself mechanical drawing while working as an office boy for a patent firm. He drafted the drawings for Bell's telephone patent. In 1882 he patented an improved process for manufacturing the carbon filaments that Edison's bulbs required, which made them cheaper and longer lasting. He later joined the Edison Electric Light Company as a draftsman and expert witness in patent cases, and he wrote one of the first practical books on electric lighting. Latimer worked at the center of the industry this lesson describes, and almost no one has heard of him.

These stories are worth holding next to Edison's. Each of these inventors solved a hard technical problem through sustained, disciplined work. Three of them had to solve a second problem as well, and that second problem had nothing to do with engineering. Some societies make certain members prove they are capable of thought before anyone will look at their evidence. A society that does this wastes exactly the talent it claims to prize. That waste is not an accident of the machinery. It is a failure of the civic culture around it, and it is fair to judge the age by it.
