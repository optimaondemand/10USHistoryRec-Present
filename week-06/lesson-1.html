📖 LESSON 6.1
Transformative Inventions and the Beginning of Consumer Society
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The same decades that built railroads and steel mills also rewired daily life at home. Alexander Graham Bell's telephone (1876) and Edison's phonograph (1877) turned communication and entertainment into technologies rather than purely human acts. Richard Sears and Montgomery Ward built mail-order catalogs that let a farm family in Kansas order manufactured goods, clothing, tools, even prefabricated houses, from a printed page and have them arrive by the same railroads carrying wheat to market. A strengthened patent system gave inventors a real financial incentive to commercialize new ideas quickly, rather than keep them as private curiosities, and it is no accident that the number of U.S. patents issued each year roughly quadrupled between 1870 and 1900.

None of this reached Americans as isolated gadgets; it arrived as an emerging culture of consumption. Department stores like Marshall Field's in Chicago and Wanamaker's in Philadelphia turned shopping itself into an experience, with fixed prices, return policies, and elaborate window displays that had no real precedent in the small, personal shops of an earlier generation. Advertising grew from simple notices into a genuine industry built on persuading people to want goods they had not previously known existed. A rising urban middle class, with steadier wages than a farmer's harvest-dependent income, became the customer base this whole system was built to serve.

The deepest change may have been to time itself. Electric lighting, spreading through American cities in the 1880s and 1890s, meant that work, shopping, and leisure no longer had to end at sunset. A city under electric light was, in a real sense, a new kind of place to live, and the invention this week's lesson takes up in detail, the incandescent light bulb, was the single technology most responsible for making it possible.
