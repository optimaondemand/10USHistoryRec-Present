The Urban Progressive Movement and the Politics of Reform

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Progressivismproh-GRESS-iv-iz-umA broad reform movement, dominant in American politics from roughly 1900 to 1917, that used expert investigation and government regulation to address industrialization's abuses. was the broad reform movement that dominated American politics from roughly 1900 to 1917. It differed from the agrarian Populism of Week 2, in both its base and its method. Populists were mostly rural farmers, organizing against railroads and currency policy. Progressives were different. They were mostly urban and middle-class: professionals, journalists, and social workers. They believed something specific. Expert investigation and government regulation, not direct political revolt, could correct the abuses industrialization had produced. Progressives did not agree on everything. Some focused on political corruption. Some focused on trust-busting, the subject of Week 13. Some focused on public health. And some, as this week's material shows, focused specifically on housing.

Progressives shared a method more than a single cause, and that method was documentation. Muckraking journalists, described in Week 7, used investigative reporting to expose corporate abuse. Settlement house workers like Jane Addams, described in Week 8, used case files and neighborhood maps to expose urban poverty. Housing reformers, this week's subject, used photography and building surveys to expose the tenement crisis. Each group trusted the same basic idea. Specific, verifiable facts, gathered patiently and presented plainly, could move public opinion and lawmakers in a way pure argument could not. Women's organizations carried much of this work forward on the ground. The National Consumers LeagueNASH-uh-nul kun-SOO-merz leegAn organization founded in 1899, led by Florence Kelley, that pushed for housing and labor standards nationally. was founded in 1899. Florence Kelley led it, the same reformer named in Week 8 for Illinois's 1893 factory law. The League pushed housing and labor standards nationally in the years that followed.

Progressive reform took different shapes in different places. In Wisconsin, Governor Robert La FolletteRAH-bert luh FAH-letThe Wisconsin governor who pioneered the "Wisconsin Idea" of bringing university experts into government to draft reform legislation. pioneered what became known as the Wisconsin Ideawis-KAHN-sin eye-DEE-uhLa Follette's approach of using university experts to draft state legislation on regulation and reform.. He brought university experts directly into state government, to draft legislation on railroad regulation, tax policy, and workplace safety. New York's housing reformers worked the same way, in their own city and their own field. Bring in the facts. Bring in the experts. Then write the facts and the expertise into enforceable law.

Housing reform grew directly out of the urbanization crisis Week 5 described. New York's population had packed itself into "dumbbell tenementsDUM-bel TEN-uh-mentA tenement building design, pinched in the middle like a dumbbell, that technically met an 1879 light-and-air law while leaving most rooms without real light or air.." These buildings were narrowed in the middle. That shape satisfied an 1879 law's light-and-air requirements on paper. But it left most interior rooms almost entirely without windows, natural light, or ventilation. That 1879 law is itself worth pausing on. It was a real reform, passed with good intentions. It produced the dumbbell design anyway, as landlords' cheapest way of technically obeying it. A law that names a standard, without funding anyone to check compliance, does not reliably produce the outcome it names. That is a lesson the Progressives who followed took directly to heart.

Picture the actual shape. A dumbbell tenement was pinched in at the sides in the middle of the building, like the bar of a dumbbell weight. That pinch created a narrow airshaft, sometimes barely two feet wide, running the full height of the building between it and its neighbor. Bedrooms opened onto that airshaft instead of onto open sky. In practice, the airshaft let in almost no light and very little air. It also became a chute for garbage, and a path for fire to travel fast between floors. The widget on this lesson's page lets you compare that layout directly against the courtyard design the 1901 law required instead.

Jacob RiisJAY-kub reessA Danish immigrant police reporter whose flash photography documented tenement conditions in his 1890 book How the Other Half Lives. was a Danish immigrant and police reporter. His own primary source this week documents tenement conditions directly. Riis used a newly available technology: flash powder photography. It let him do what earlier written reports alone had not. In his 1890 book How the Other Half Liveshow thuh UH-ther haf livzJacob Riis's 1890 photographic book documenting New York tenement conditions., he showed middle-class readers exactly what a windowless tenement interior actually looked like. The camera could go where written statistics could not, into a dark airshaft bedroom at midnight. The material fact of flash photography's recent invention, not any new argument about poverty, made that kind of documentation possible for the first time. Reformers, social workers, and public health officials had watched tuberculosis and infant mortality rates climb in these buildings. They combined Riis's images with a method Ida Tarbell had already used against Standard Oil, back in Week 7: specific addresses, specific numbers, specific families. It was an argument landlords' lobbying could not simply wave away with generalities.

That documentation effort had real organizers behind it. Robert W. De ForestRAH-bert dee FOR-estA lawyer and reformer who chaired the 1900 New York commission investigating tenement conditions. was a lawyer and charity reformer. He chaired a state commission created in 1900 to investigate tenement conditions across New York City. Lawrence VeillerLOR-ens VY-lerA housing reformer who drafted the Tenement House Act of 1901 and founded the National Housing Association. was a young housing reformer who served as the commission's secretary. He drafted most of its final report, and the legislative language that followed from it. Veiller went on to found the National Housing Association several years later. Tenement reform became something close to a full career for him, not a single campaign.

That campaign succeeded. New York's Tenement House Act of 1901 is this week's discussion topic, and the subject of the next lesson. It replaced the dumbbell design with new construction standards. It required real interior courtyards, indoor plumbing on every floor, and fire escapes. It also created a dedicated city department, with the power to inspect and enforce those standards, the very enforcement machinery the 1879 law had lacked. It stands as one of the clearest examples in this unit of a specific, well-documented problem translating directly into a specific, enforceable law. That is exactly the kind of nongovernmental pressure shaping public policy this course's standards ask students to examine. It is also a reminder that reform in this period rarely arrived on the first attempt.

None of this happened without real people making real choices, at some cost to themselves. Riis spent years walking tenement blocks at night, in neighborhoods that most of his own newspaper's readers never entered. De Forest gave a lawyer's working years to a state commission instead of private practice. Veiller built an entire career around one specific, unglamorous cause: enforceable housing codes. Progressivism can sound abstract when it is described only as a movement or an era. It was also just these three men, and many others like them, doing patient, specific work.
