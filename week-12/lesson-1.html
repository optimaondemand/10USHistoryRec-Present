📖 LESSON 12.1
The Urban Progressive Movement and the Politics of Reform
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Progressivism, the broad reform movement that dominated American politics from roughly 1900 to 1917, differed from the agrarian Populism of Week 2 in both its base and its method. Where Populists were mostly rural farmers organizing against railroads and currency policy, Progressives were mostly urban and middle-class, professionals, journalists, and social workers who believed that expert investigation and government regulation, rather than direct political revolt, could correct the abuses industrialization had produced. Progressives did not agree on everything; some focused on political corruption, some on trust-busting (Week 13), some on public health, and some, as this week's material shows, specifically on housing.

Housing reform grew directly out of the urbanization crisis Week 5 described. New York's population had packed itself into "dumbbell tenements," buildings narrowed in the middle to satisfy an 1879 law's light-and-air requirements on paper while leaving most interior rooms almost entirely without windows, natural light, or ventilation. Reformers, social workers, and public health officials who had watched tuberculosis and infant mortality rates climb in these buildings began documenting conditions in exhaustive, specific detail, following the same evidentiary method Tarbell had used against Standard Oil, in order to make an argument for stricter building law that landlords' lobbying could not simply wave away.

That documentation succeeded. New York's Tenement House Act of 1901, this week's discussion topic, replaced the dumbbell design with new construction standards requiring real interior courtyards, indoor plumbing on every floor, and fire escapes, and created a dedicated city department with the power to inspect and enforce those standards. It stands as one of the clearest examples in this unit of a specific, well-documented problem translating directly into a specific, enforceable law, exactly the kind of nongovernmental pressure shaping public policy this course's standards ask students to examine.
