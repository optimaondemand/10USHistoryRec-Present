Reconstruction: Political Settlement and New Constitutions in the South
📖 LESSON 1
The Political Settlement of Reconstruction
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On the night of April 14, 1865, an actor's bullet did more to reshape the next twelve years of American history than any single law Congress would pass. Abraham Lincoln had spent the war's final weeks sketching a lenient peace: swift readmission for the seceded states, minimal punishment, "malice toward none, charity for all." Had he lived out his second term, Reconstruction might have looked very different. Instead the office fell to Vice President Andrew Johnson, a Tennessee Democrat who had stayed loyal to the Union but shared little of Lincoln's magnanimity toward the freedpeople whose future now hung on decisions made in Washington.

Johnson pressed ahead with a plan nearly as lenient as Lincoln's: pardon most Confederates, restore state governments quickly, and leave the terms of Black civil life to the states themselves. The Southern legislatures elected under this plan answered by passing the Black Codes, laws that criminalized unemployment for freedpeople, restricted their right to own land or testify against white citizens, and bound many to labor contracts that looked, in practice, very much like the slavery the war had just abolished. To the Republicans who controlled Congress, this looked like the Confederacy attempting to win on paper what it had lost on the battlefield.

What followed was one of the sharpest constitutional confrontations between a president and Congress in American history. A faction known as the Radical Republicans, led by Thaddeus Stevens in the House and Charles Sumner in the Senate, argued that the defeated South had forfeited any claim to prompt readmission, and that Congress, not the president, held the constitutional authority to set the terms of reunion. Johnson vetoed the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the renewal of the Freedmen's Bureau; Congress overrode him on both, something that had almost never happened to an American president before. The break was now open, and it would end in Johnson's impeachment by the House in 1868 (he survived removal in the Senate by a single vote).

Congress's answer took constitutional form in the 14th Amendment, ratified in 1868, which made citizenship and equal protection under the law a guarantee no state could withdraw. When several Southern states refused to ratify it, Congress passed the Reconstruction Act of 1867 over Johnson's veto, dividing the former Confederacy into five military districts and requiring each state to write a new constitution guaranteeing Black male suffrage before it could send representatives back to Washington. By 1868, new state governments across the South, elected under these rules and including Black officeholders for the first time in American history, had begun the work of rebuilding.

None of this was inevitable. A surviving Lincoln, a more accommodating Congress, or a different set of choices by Southern legislatures in 1865 and 1866 could each have produced a milder, faster reunification with far less direct federal supervision of the South. That it went the way it did was the product of specific decisions, made by specific men, in a narrow window of years.
