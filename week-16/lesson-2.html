📖 TODAY'S LESSON

William Jennings BryanWIL-yum JEN-ingz BRY-unA three-time Democratic presidential candidate, orator, and fundamentalist who combined economic populism, pacifism, and religious traditionalism. burst onto the national stage at the 1896 Democratic National Convention. He was thirty-six years old. Before that summer, he was a former one-term congressman from Nebraska, barely known outside his home state. He had trained as a lawyer in Illinois before moving west to Lincoln to build a legal and political career. The convention itself was already split. President Grover Cleveland, a Democrat, backed a currency tied only to gold. A rebel faction of Southern and Western Democrats wanted something different. They wanted silver coined freely alongside gold, at a fixed ratio of sixteen ounces of silver to one ounce of gold. That was the same demand Week 2's Grange and Farmers' Alliance descendants had raised for years. Free silver, they argued, would ease the debt crushing indebted farmers. It would also lift crop prices battered by years of deflation.

Bryan rose to close the currency debate. He delivered the most famous speech in the history of American party conventions. He closed with a line that turned the hall into an uproar: "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold," a line that gave the address its lasting name, the "Cross of Gold" speechkraws uv gohld speechWilliam Jennings Bryan's 1896 speech opposing the gold standard, which won him the Democratic presidential nomination at age thirty-six.. The convention nominated him for president the very next day. That made him the youngest major-party presidential nominee in American history. The People's Party, the Populists this course met in Week 2, faced a hard choice of their own. They could run their own candidate, or endorse Bryan and fold their movement into his. They chose to endorse him. That fusion decision gave Bryan a bigger coalition in 1896. It also cost the Populists their independent identity afterward, a genuine trade-off historians still debate.

Bryan lost that November to William McKinley. McKinley's campaign manager, Marcus Hanna, ran a disciplined, heavily funded operation. McKinley greeted supporters from his own front porch in Canton, Ohio, while Hanna's operation flooded the country with pamphlets and paid speakers. Bryan, by contrast, crossed the country by train, giving hundreds of speeches directly to crowds himself. He ran for president twice more, in 1900 and 1908, and lost both times. In 1900, he added a second major issue: opposition to American imperialism in the Philippines, the subject of Week 9. No other figure, winning or losing, did more to fuse agrarian economic protest with a specifically religious moral vocabulary, "crown of thorns," "crucify," in mainstream American politics.

That same religious confidence carried Bryan through the rest of a long public career. In 1901, he founded a weekly newspaper called The Commonerthuh KAH-muh-nerA weekly newspaper founded by Bryan in 1901 to keep his political voice alive between campaigns. to keep his political voice alive between campaigns. He also toured the country as a paid lecturer, one of the most sought-after public speakers of his era. In 1913, Woodrow Wilson made him Secretary of State. Bryan negotiated a series of "cooling-off" treaties as part of that job. These treaties required nations to submit disputes to a period of investigation before declaring war, a diplomatic project matching his pacifist convictions directly. He resigned that post in June 1915, as this week's first primary source shows. The break came over Wilson's second official protest note to Germany after the Lusitanialoo-sih-TAY-nee-uhA British ocean liner sunk by a German submarine in May 1915, killing 128 Americans and straining U.S. neutrality. sinking. Bryan judged the note's tone too provocative. He believed it made war with Germany more likely, not less. He resigned on principle, not for any political advantage, a costly and public break with the president who had appointed him.

Bryan spent his final years as the nation's most visible defender of Christian fundamentalism. That defense reached its climax ten years after his State Department resignation. In 1925, Bryan traveled to Dayton, Tennessee, for the Scopes trialskohps trylA 1925 Tennessee trial over a law banning the teaching of evolution, in which Bryan argued the prosecution's case and was cross-examined by Clarence Darrow., this week's second primary source. He argued the prosecution's case against teaching evolution in the state's public schools, under a new law called the Butler ActBUT-ler aktThe Tennessee law banning the teaching of evolution in public schools, at issue in the Scopes trial.. The trial became a national sensation, drawing reporters from across the country and, for the first time in American history, a live radio broadcast of courtroom testimony. Defense attorney Clarence DarrowKLAIR-uns DAIR-ohThe defense attorney in the Scopes trial who questioned Bryan on the witness stand about the Bible. represented the schoolteacher John Scopes. In a dramatic move, Darrow called Bryan himself to the witness stand as an expert on the Bible. Darrow's sharp questioning left Bryan flustered on several specific points of scripture. National newspapers, led by the mocking dispatches of journalist H. L. Menckenmengk-enA journalist whose mocking coverage of the Scopes trial helped give it the nickname "the Monkey Trial.", covered the exchange without mercy. Reporters nicknamed the whole affair the "Monkey Trial," mocking the idea that humans and apes shared a common ancestor. The jury convicted Scopes and fined him one hundred dollars. Tennessee's Supreme Court later threw out that fine on a technicality, without ever overturning the Butler Act itself, so the underlying question the trial had promised to settle stayed legally unresolved. Bryan did not live to see even that partial outcome. He died just five days after the trial ended, still in Dayton.

What makes Bryan historiographically useful is precisely that he does not sort neatly into any modern political label. He was, at once, an economic populist who distrusted concentrated wealth. He was also a pacifist who resigned a powerful office rather than risk war. And he was a religious traditionalist who fought the teaching of evolution in public schools. A modern reader might expect these three positions scattered across different political camps. For Bryan, they cohered into one consistent worldview. At its center stood a single aim: protecting ordinary people from Wall Street, from war, and from what he saw as a corrosive secular science. Judged in Classical Republican terms, Bryan's record shows real virtue and a real cost sitting side by side. His courage in defending unpopular convictions, at his own political expense, three times over, was genuine. So was the rigidity that same certainty produced, once Darrow finally put it to the test in front of a national audience. A civic culture, in the Classical Republican framework this course applies throughout, succeeds partly by restraining appetite and binding citizens to a common good. Bryan spent thirty years arguing that ordinary farmers, ordinary wage earners, and ordinary churchgoers deserved exactly that kind of protection against concentrated power, whether that power sat on Wall Street, in a war department, or in a university science department. He never won the presidency. But few Americans of his generation shaped public argument, on so many separate fronts, as thoroughly as he did.
