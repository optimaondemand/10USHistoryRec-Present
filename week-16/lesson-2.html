📖 LESSON 16.3
William Jennings Bryan and the Cross of Gold
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

William Jennings Bryan burst onto the national stage at the 1896 Democratic National Convention with a speech defending the free coinage of silver, the Populist-adjacent monetary policy Week 2's Grange and Farmers' Alliance descendants had championed as relief from a gold-backed currency that kept debts heavy and crop prices low. Bryan closed with a line that made him, at thirty-six, the youngest major-party presidential nominee in American history: "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." He would run for president three times, in 1896, 1900, and 1908, and lose all three, but no other figure did more to fuse agrarian economic protest with a specifically religious moral vocabulary, "crown of thorns," "crucify," in mainstream American politics.

That same religious confidence carried Bryan through a long public career: newspaper editor, three-time presidential candidate, and eventually Woodrow Wilson's Secretary of State from 1913 to 1915, a post from which he resigned, as Tuesday's primary source shows, on a matter of principle rather than political convenience. He would spend his final years as the nation's most visible defender of Christian fundamentalism, culminating in his role, ten years after his State Department resignation, in the 1925 Scopes trial, Thursday's primary source, where he argued the prosecution's case against teaching evolution in Tennessee's public schools.

What makes Bryan historiographically useful is precisely that he does not sort neatly into any modern political label. He was, at once, an economic populist who distrusted concentrated wealth, a pacifist who resigned rather than risk war, and a religious traditionalist who fought the teaching of evolution, three positions a modern reader might expect to find scattered across different political camps, but which cohered for Bryan into a single, consistent worldview centered on protecting ordinary people, whether from Wall Street, from war, or from what he saw as a corrosive secular science.
