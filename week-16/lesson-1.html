Fundamentalism and Pacifism

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Protestant fundamentalismPRAH-tes-tunt fun-duh-MEN-tuh-liz-umA conservative Christian movement defending core doctrines against theological modernism and Darwinian evolution. did not appear out of nowhere. It grew as a direct reaction against two currents this course has already traced. The first was theological modernismthee-uh-LAH-jih-kul MAH-der-niz-umA movement to reinterpret Christian doctrine in light of modern science and biblical criticism.. Modernist ministers wanted to reinterpret Christian doctrine in light of modern science and biblical criticism. The second was Darwinian evolution. Evolution challenged a literal reading of Genesis, the Bible's opening book. Many devout Protestants felt both currents were eroding the faith's foundations from within.

Between 1910 and 1915, two California oil businessmen answered back. Lyman and Milton Stewart funded a series of pamphlets called The Fundamentalsthuh fun-duh-MEN-tulzA series of pamphlets (1910-1915) defending core Christian doctrines, which gave the fundamentalist movement its name.. Editors A. C. Dixon and R. A. Torrey gathered essays from ninety conservative Protestant scholars. The pamphlets defended core Christian doctrines. Biblical inerrancyBIB-lih-kul in-AIR-un-seeThe belief that scripture contains no errors., the belief that scripture contains no errors, stood at the center. Publishers mailed millions of free copies to pastors, missionaries, and seminary students across the English-speaking world. The movement later took its lasting name directly from this project. In 1920, a Baptist editor named Curtis Lee Laws coined the word "fundamentalist." He meant Christians ready, in his phrase, "to do battle royal" for these doctrines.

Fundamentalists did not reject modern life as a whole. Many used up-to-date printing, mailing lists, and even radio to spread their message. What they refused was the idea that secular scholarship could simply overrule scripture. Some religious truths, in their view, were not open to revision, no matter how modern learning changed.

The First World War itself pushed many Americans toward fundamentalist confidence. The Social Gospel movement, covered in Week 8, preached an optimistic, this-worldly Christianity. Reformers believed steady moral progress could bring God's kingdom to earth through social reform. The war's staggering violence shook that optimism hard. If human civilization could produce a conflict this brutal, many asked, how much confidence should anyone place in human progress? Fundamentalist preachers held a more pessimistic view of human nature. Amid the war's devastation, they found a wider audience than they had found before it.

Part of the disagreement was theological, not just emotional. Social Gospel reformers tended toward postmillennialismpohst-mih-LEN-ee-uh-liz-umThe belief that human effort could gradually build a better world before Christ's return., the belief that human effort could gradually build a better world before Christ's promised return. Many fundamentalists instead held to premillennialismpree-mih-LEN-ee-uh-liz-umThe belief that the world would grow worse until Christ returned to set it right by divine action., the belief that the world would grow worse, not better, until Christ returned to set it right by divine action alone. A war that killed millions across four years gave the second view a grim kind of confirmation the first view struggled to explain away. Neither side treated the dispute as a small one. Each read the same headlines as evidence for its own account of where history was actually headed.

Woodrow Wilson himself stood outside this argument but was shaped by faith all the same. He was the son and grandson of Presbyterian ministers, and he served for years as a Presbyterian church elder. Wilson was neither a fundamentalist nor a Social Gospel radical. But his own confidence that America carried a moral mission to the world, the conviction this course will meet again in Week 18's Fourteen Points, drew directly on that religious formation. Ideas about scripture, providence, and national purpose were not confined to preachers and pamphlets in these years. They shaped how a sitting president understood America's role in the largest war the world had yet seen.

Christian pacifismKRIS-chun PAS-ih-fiz-umThe belief that the New Testament forbids Christians from waging war. ran on a related but separate track. Pacifism held that the New Testament forbade Christians from waging war. It gained a wide hearing as war engulfed Europe after 1914. American pacifists came from across the theological map. Jane Addams, the Chicago reformer this course met in Week 12 through her Hull House settlement work, helped found the Woman's Peace PartyWOO-munz peess PAR-teeA pacifist organization co-founded by Jane Addams in 1915. in 1915. Her pacifism grew from progressive social ethics, not from biblical literalism. She believed war destroyed exactly the kind of community cooperation reform work was trying to build. In 1915, Addams traveled to the Netherlands to chair an International Congress of Women at The Hague, meeting with reformers from both warring and neutral nations at once. The American Union Against Militarism, a separate coalition founded that same year, lobbied Congress directly against Preparedness Movement spending, the buildup this course will examine in the next lesson. Addams paid a real cost for this stance. Her once-enormous public popularity, built over decades of settlement-house work, dropped sharply once she opposed a war most of her fellow reformers came to support.

William Jennings BryanWIL-yum JEN-ingz BRY-unA three-time Democratic presidential candidate, orator, and fundamentalist who combined economic populism, pacifism, and religious traditionalism., this week's central figure, reached the same pacifist conclusion by a very different road. Bryan combined pacifist conviction with the same fundamentalist confidence in scripture that produced The Fundamentals. If the Gospel plainly taught peace, Bryan reasoned, then no argument about national interest or military necessity could override it. National leaders, in his view, owed obedience to scripture above any calculation of strategic advantage.

Fundamentalism and pacifism are not the same movement. Most fundamentalists were not committed pacifists. The popular revivalist Billy Sunday, for instance, preached fierce biblical literalism while cheering loudly for American entry into the war. Most pacifists, in turn, were not theological fundamentalists; Addams's own case proves that plainly. But in Bryan, the two convictions sat together without any sense of contradiction. Both rested, in his mind, on the same confident, literal reading of scripture as a reliable guide to modern moral and political questions.

That coherence is worth pausing on in Classical Republican terms. A civic culture succeeds partly by cultivating habits that restrain appetite and bind citizens to a common good. Bryan believed scripture supplied exactly those restraining habits, for individuals and for nations alike. The same confidence that let Bryan resign a cabinet post on principle also left him little room to doubt himself later, in a courtroom where doubt might have served him well. Whether that belief made him a wiser statesman or simply a more rigid one is a real question. This week's two primary sources, written a decade apart on entirely different subjects, let you examine that question directly. One document shows Bryan defending peace from inside Wilson's own cabinet. The other shows him defending scripture from a Tennessee courtroom. Together, they show one man applying a single conviction to two very different fights.
