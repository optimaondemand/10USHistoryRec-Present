📖 LESSON 16.1
Fundamentalism and Pacifism
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Protestant fundamentalism emerged in these decades as a direct reaction against two currents this course has already traced: theological modernism, which sought to reinterpret Christian doctrine in light of modern science and biblical criticism, and Darwinian evolution, which challenged a literal reading of Genesis. Between 1910 and 1915, a series of pamphlets titled The Fundamentals laid out a defense of core Christian doctrines, biblical inerrancy chief among them, that gave the movement its lasting name. Fundamentalists did not reject modern life wholesale, but they insisted that certain religious truths were not open to revision simply because secular scholarship had changed.

Christian pacifism, the conviction that the New Testament's teaching forbade Christians from waging war, ran on a related but distinct track, and it gained a wide hearing as war engulfed Europe after 1914. American pacifists came from across the theological spectrum, but many of the most prominent, including this week's primary source author, combined pacifist conviction with the same fundamentalist confidence in scripture's clear moral authority: if the Gospel plainly taught peace, no argument of national interest or military necessity could override it.

These two movements, fundamentalism and pacifism, are not identical, and most fundamentalists were not committed pacifists nor were most pacifists theological fundamentalists. But William Jennings Bryan held both convictions at once, and neither felt to him like an odd combination: both rested, in his mind, on the same confident, literal reading of scripture as a reliable guide to modern moral and political questions, a coherence this week's two primary sources, written a decade apart on entirely different subjects, will let you examine directly.
