World War I: American Mobilization and Entry
📖 LESSON 1
American Mobilization and Entry
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Two specific events, not a slow inevitable drift, brought the United States from the neutrality of Week 17 into the war. In January 1917, British intelligence intercepted and decoded the Zimmermann Telegram, in which Germany's foreign secretary proposed a secret alliance with Mexico, promising Mexico the return of Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona if it would attack the United States should America enter the war. Weeks later, Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare against all shipping in British waters, including neutral American vessels, a policy it had suspended after the Lusitania crisis (Week 16) precisely to avoid provoking American entry. Faced with both a direct threat and a direct provocation within a matter of weeks, Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war on April 2, 1917, arguing "the world must be made safe for democracy," and Congress obliged four days later.

Turning a nation of roughly four million active and reserve soldiers, mostly untrained, into a force capable of fighting in France required an entirely new mobilization apparatus. The Selective Service Act of May 1917 authorized the first American conscription since the Civil War, eventually registering some twenty-four million men. The War Industries Board coordinated industrial production for the war effort, Liberty Bonds financed it by selling war debt directly to ordinary citizens, and the Committee on Public Information, a government propaganda agency, worked to build enthusiastic public support, sometimes shading into outright suppression of dissent under the Espionage and Sedition Acts, which the course will examine further in Spring semester.

By late 1917 and through 1918, roughly two million American soldiers, the American Expeditionary Forces, deployed to France under General John J. Pershing, arriving in numbers large enough, alongside a battered but intact French and British line, to help break the German army's last major offensive in the spring of 1918 and force an armistice that November. American losses, roughly 116,000 dead, were far smaller than the millions lost by the war's other major combatants, but the shock of even that scale of loss, concentrated into barely a year and a half of active combat, transformed a country that had watched the war from a distance for nearly three years.
