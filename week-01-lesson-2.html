This Week's Topic
Reconstruction: Political Settlement and New Constitutions in the South
📖 LESSON 1.3
The Grant Presidency
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Ulysses S. Grant won the presidency in 1868 as the general who had brought the Confederacy to surrender, and he brought a soldier's directness to the office. When the Ku Klux Klan rose up across the South to terrorize Black voters and the white Republicans who supported them, Grant backed the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871, which made interference with voting rights a federal crime and let him suspend habeas corpus in parts of South Carolina to break the Klan's power there. For a few years, federal troops and federal marshals made Black political participation in the South a genuine, if fragile, reality: by the early 1870s, more than a dozen Black congressmen and hundreds of Black state legislators had been elected across the former Confederacy.

Grant's own reputation for personal honesty was not in question. But he trusted subordinates freely and investigated them reluctantly, and his administration became a byword for scandal: the Crédit Mobilier affair, in which railroad insiders bribed congressmen with discounted stock; the Whiskey Ring, in which distillers and Treasury officials conspired to skim millions in tax revenue; and an Indian trading-post scandal that reached his own secretary of war. None of these scandals touched Grant's personal integrity directly, but each one gave Northern voters one more reason to tire of a federal government that seemed permanently occupied with Southern enforcement and permanently embarrassed by its own corruption.

That fatigue mattered more than any single scandal. An economic depression after the Panic of 1873 pushed voters to care more about jobs than about federal troops in South Carolina. Northern Republicans who had supported Reconstruction on principle began to ask how much longer the North's soldiers and patience should be spent on the South's internal politics. By Grant's second term, Southern Democrats calling themselves "Redeemers" were already retaking state governments one by one, sometimes by ballot and sometimes by the same paramilitary violence the Enforcement Acts had been written to stop. The political will to finish what the Reconstruction Act of 1867 had started was running out in the North well before it ran out in law.
