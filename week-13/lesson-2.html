📖 LESSON 13.3
JP Morgan and Northern Securities
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By 1901, J.P. Morgan was already the most powerful banker in America. He had spent the 1890s reorganizing bankrupt railroads, a process people called "Morganization." He would take a failing line, cut its debt, and install his own men on its board. His influence ran through dozens of American railroads at once, often through interlocking directors who sat on several boards together. Northern Securities grew directly out of that same world.

In the spring of 1901, railroad magnates James J. Hill and E.H. Harriman fought for control of the Northern Pacific Railroad. Harriman, backed by the banking house Kuhn, Loeb and Company and by Standard Oil money, began secretly buying up Northern Pacific stock. He hoped to seize the line before Hill and Morgan noticed. They noticed in time, and both sides raced to buy every remaining share. On May 9, 1901, the price of Northern Pacific common stock spiked from around one hundred fourteen dollars to a thousand dollars in a single morning. Investors who had sold the stock short, betting its price would fall, were suddenly ruined. The spike briefly panicked the entire stock market. Hill and Morgan ended up with control, but the fight had come dangerously close to wrecking Wall Street by accident.

Hill and Harriman were not strangers fighting over an abstract prize. Hill had built the Great Northern Railway without a single federal land grant. That was unusual for a transcontinental line, and Hill took genuine pride in it. Newspapers had nicknamed him the "Empire Builder" for the achievement. Harriman had made his own reputation rescuing the bankrupt Union Pacific a few years earlier. He had turned it into one of the best-run railroads in the country almost from nothing. Both men were skilled, ambitious, and used to winning. Neither was willing to simply let the other seize a railroad he considered his own.

To prevent anything like it from happening again, the rivals agreed to fold both the Northern Pacific and the Great Northern into one new holding company. They called it the Northern Securities Company, chartered under New Jersey's lenient 1889 holding-company law, the same law this course met in Week 4. The plan made perfect sense to the financiers involved. It eliminated, at a single stroke, all competition between two of the most important rail lines in the northern United States. Few observers expected anyone to challenge it.

Roosevelt's Justice Department sued to dissolve the company in 1902, under the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890. It was a genuinely bold move against the most powerful banker in the country. Most lawyers at the time assumed the Sherman Act applied only to obvious price-fixing schemes, like the trusts this course examined in Week 4, not to a legal merger of stock ownership. Morgan, hearing of the suit, reportedly told Roosevelt he should simply "send your man to my man" and settle the matter privately, the way powerful men had always settled disputes between themselves. Morgan missed the point entirely. Roosevelt intended to fight this out in open court, as a matter of public law, not behind closed doors.

Attorney General Philander Knox filed the suit without warning Morgan in advance, which was itself part of the message. The case, Northern Securities Company v. United States, reached the Supreme Court in 1904. The Court split five to four, the narrowest possible margin, but it upheld the government's case. Justice John Marshall Harlan wrote the majority opinion. Harlan was the same justice who, eight years earlier, had written the lone dissent in Plessy v. Ferguson, the case this course studied back in Week 8. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes wrote one of the dissents. He warned that "great cases, like hard cases, make bad law." An unusual, high-profile case, Holmes meant, can tempt judges to bend a legal principle out of shape just to reach a satisfying result in that one instance. The majority disagreed with Holmes's caution. It ruled that a merger did not have to look like an explicit price-fixing agreement to violate the Sherman Act. What mattered was its practical effect. If a combination destroyed competition between companies that would otherwise compete, its legal form as stock ownership rather than a formal cartel made no difference at all.

It was Roosevelt's first great trust-busting victory as president, and it established a legal principle that antitrust law has relied on ever since: substance, not legal form, determines whether a combination illegally restrains trade. The victory was not quite as complete as it looked, however. The Court ordered Northern Securities dissolved, and it was. Hill and Morgan kept effective control of both railroads anyway. They simply arranged for the same small group of stockholders to hold shares in each company separately, rather than through one combined holding company. No law forced those individual stockholders to vote their two railroads against each other. The underlying economic problem, the danger that these two lines might quietly stop competing, was addressed more by public attention than by any permanent structural fix. Even so, the case sent an unmistakable signal that no combination, however large or however well connected, stood automatically above the reach of federal law.

Roosevelt's reputation as "the trust-buster" outlived the details of any single case, including this one, and it is worth checking that reputation against the actual record. Roosevelt filed more than forty antitrust suits over nearly eight years in office. His successor, William Howard Taft, is usually remembered as the more cautious, more conservative of the two men, closer to big business and less inclined to confrontation. Yet Taft's Justice Department filed roughly twice as many antitrust suits in a single four-year term. Roosevelt had needed almost twice that many years to file his forty. Taft's suits included the one that finally broke up Standard Oil, decided by the Supreme Court in 1911, a case this course has already followed from Rockefeller's own side back in Week 4. Reputation and record do not always match. Northern Securities mattered less because it was the largest or most thorough of these cases and more because it was the first, and because it proved a sitting president could take on Wall Street's most powerful man and win.
