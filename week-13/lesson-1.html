📖 LESSON 13.1
Trust-Busting, Regulation, Conservation, and the Pure Food and Drug Act
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Roosevelt's federal Square Deal reached considerably further than the coal strike arbitration of Week 7. His administration filed more than forty antitrust suits under the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, a law that had sat mostly unenforced for its first decade, and pushed through the Elkins Act (1903) and Hepburn Act (1906), which finally gave the federal Interstate Commerce Commission real power to set maximum railroad freight rates, closing the loophole that had let railroads offer secret rebates to favored shippers like Rockefeller's Standard Oil back in Week 4.

The most direct legislative consequence of this unit's earlier material came in 1906, when Congress passed the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act within months of each other, a direct government response to the public outrage Upton Sinclair's The Jungle had provoked in Week 7, even though Sinclair's own intended target had been labor conditions rather than food safety. The Meat Inspection Act required federal inspectors in every meatpacking plant engaged in interstate commerce; the Pure Food and Drug Act banned the interstate sale of mislabeled or adulterated food and medicine for the first time in American history.

Roosevelt also began a conservation program this course examines fully in Week 15, setting aside roughly 230 million acres of public land as national forests, parks, and monuments during his presidency, treating natural resources, like food safety and fair competition, as a public good the federal government had a legitimate role in protecting from purely private exploitation. Taken together, these were not isolated policies but a single consistent philosophy: that unregulated economic power, whether over railroads, food, or forests, would not police itself, and that only a federal government willing to act as referee could guarantee the "square deal" Roosevelt had promised.
