📖 LESSON 12.3
The Tenement House Act of 1901
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

New York's Tenement House Act, signed into law in April 1901 after years of reformer pressure, was the direct legislative descendant of the documentation campaign Monday's lesson described. It outlawed the dumbbell design for new construction, requiring genuinely open courtyards rather than narrow airshafts, mandated a toilet connected to a water and sewer system on every floor rather than a single outhouse shared by an entire building, required fire escapes on every tenement, and created a new city agency, the Tenement House Department, staffed with inspectors empowered to enter buildings and issue enforceable violations.

The Act's most important feature may have been this last one: enforcement. Earlier tenement laws, including the 1879 law the dumbbell design had been built to technically satisfy, had specified standards without giving any agency real power or funding to inspect buildings and compel compliance. The 1901 law's inspectors could, and did, force landlords to make expensive retrofits to existing buildings, not merely apply the new standard to future construction, a genuinely significant expansion of the reach of city government into what had previously been treated as purely private property.

The law did not end tenement poverty, and Riis's own photographs, the subject of Thursday's primary source, had already been taken before it passed; no single act of legislation could undo decades of overcrowded urban growth overnight. But it marked a turning point in what American city government was willing to claim the authority to regulate, part of the same expansion of state power over private economic activity this unit will trace further in Week 13's trust-busting and Pure Food and Drug Act.
