📖 LESSON 9.3
Joseph Pulitzer and Yellow Journalism
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Joseph Pulitzer's New York World and William Randolph Hearst's New York Journal were locked in a circulation war throughout the 1890s, and Cuba's rebellion against Spain gave both papers exactly the kind of dramatic, morally clear story that sold copies: brave rebels, cruel Spanish oppressors, and American readers eager for both entertainment and a cause. The papers' coverage, illustrated with dramatic (and sometimes invented) engravings, earned the label "yellow journalism," after a popular comic strip character, and historians still debate how much genuine influence this coverage had on the decision for war versus how much it simply amplified a public mood that already existed.

One story about this rivalry has become so famous that it is worth treating with real caution: the claim that Hearst telegraphed an artist in Cuba, "You furnish the pictures, I'll furnish the war." No verified original of this telegram has ever surfaced, and the story may be a later invention, possibly even one Hearst's own rivals spread to discredit him. Whether or not Hearst ever wrote that exact sentence, the deeper lesson is a genuinely important one: a dramatic quotation repeated often enough can come to feel like established fact even when no one can actually verify it, which is precisely the kind of claim a careful student of history has to learn to test rather than repeat. Appendix A returns to this problem directly, using this same episode to build the skill of evaluating a source's reliability.
