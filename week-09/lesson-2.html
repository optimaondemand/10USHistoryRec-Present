Joseph Pulitzer and Yellow Journalism

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Joseph PulitzerJOH-sef PUL-it-zerA newspaper publisher whose New York World pioneered sensational, high-circulation journalism and later funded the Pulitzer Prizes. arrived in the United States from Hungary in 1864 and enlisted in a Union cavalry regiment. He learned English partly in the Army and partly in St. Louis newsrooms. He bought a failing St. Louis paper in 1878 and built the Post-Dispatch out of it. In 1883 he bought the New York World from Jay Gould, the railroad speculator of lesson 3.3. Its circulation was about fifteen thousand. Within a decade the World was selling several hundred thousand copies a day.

His formula is worth naming, because it was not simply noise. Pulitzer used big headlines, illustrations, sports, and short sentences aimed at immigrant readers learning English. He also ran genuine campaigns against corrupt officials and dishonest landlords. In 1885 Congress would not pay for the Statue of Liberty's pedestal. The World raised the money instead, from more than a hundred twenty thousand small donors, and printed their names in the paper. In 1887 the paper sent Nellie BlyNEL-ee blyA reporter for Pulitzer's World who went undercover in a New York asylum, exposing patient abuse. undercover into a New York asylum, and her account of what was done to patients there forced an official investigation. A newspaper could sell copies and serve the public in the same edition.

William Randolph HearstWIL-yum RAN-dolf herstA newspaper publisher whose New York Journal competed fiercely with Pulitzer's World, especially in covering Cuba. bought the New York Journal in 1895 with money from his family's mines. He cut the price to a penny, spent freely, and hired Pulitzer's best people away, including most of the Sunday staff. Among them was Richard Outcault, who drew a popular comic character called the Yellow Kid. Pulitzer put another artist to work drawing his own version of the same character. Two Yellow Kids in two competing papers gave the whole style its lasting name: yellow journalismYEL-oh JER-nuh-liz-umSensational, exaggerated newspaper reporting named after the "Yellow Kid" comic character both papers ran during their circulation war..

Cuba was the story both men wanted. It offered brave rebels, cruel oppressors, suffering civilians, and a moral verdict readers could reach before breakfast. Hearst sent correspondents and illustrators, among them Frederic RemingtonFRED-rik REM-ing-tunAn illustrator Hearst sent to Cuba to cover the rebellion against Spain.. In 1897 the Journal arranged a young Cuban woman's escape from a Havana jail and printed the rescue as its own achievement. It published the stolen De Lome letterduh lohm LET-erA stolen private letter by Spain's minister to Washington calling President McKinley weak, published by Hearst's newspaper in 1898.. Some engravings showed scenes no artist had witnessed. During the war both papers passed a million copies a day.

One story about this rivalry is so famous that it needs to be handled carefully. The claim is that Hearst telegraphed Remington in Cuba: "You furnish the pictures and I'll furnish the war." Here is the whole evidence base for it. The line first appears in a memoir published in 1901 by James Creelman, a journalist who had worked for both papers. Creelman does not say how he came to know of the exchange. No copy of the telegram has ever been produced. Hearst denied sending it. Spanish authorities controlled and read the cable traffic out of Havana. That makes such a message an odd thing to send, and an odder thing to go unremarked at the time.

So what should a student do with it? Not repeat it as fact, and not pretend it never circulated. It is best held as a story people told about Hearst, one that spread because it matched what his critics already believed about him. That is a useful thing to notice on its own. A quotation that fits our expectations gets repeated without checking, precisely because it fits.

The test to apply is short, and it works on any quotation. Who reported it? When, relative to the event? How would that person have known? Does the original document survive anywhere? And who benefited from the story being believed? Appendix A returns to this same episode to build that skill properly.

Now the larger question: did the yellow press cause the war? Historians disagree, and the reasons on both sides are worth knowing. The World and the Journal were New York papers. Most Americans in 1898 read a local paper that carried none of this material. Historians who have looked for a link between a district's newspapers and its representative's war vote have generally found a weak one. And the underlying facts were not invented: Weyler's camps were real, and so was the wreck in Havana harbor.

On the other side, these two papers shaped what the political class in New York and Washington read every morning. They supplied the war's vocabulary and kept Cuba on the front page for three years, which is itself a kind of power. The honest answer is that the press was one cause among several and probably not the decisive one. That is less satisfying than either "the newspapers caused the war" or "the newspapers did not matter," and it is closer to the truth. Learning to prefer the less satisfying answer when the evidence supports it is most of what historical judgment consists of.

Judge the two men by a civic standard. A republic depends on citizens who can deliberate, and deliberation depends on an information supply they can trust. By that measure the circulation war did real damage. It taught a mass readership that the vivid and the true were the same thing, and it made accuracy a competitive disadvantage.

Both men were capable of better, and Pulitzer proved it. He pulled back from the worst of the sensationalism in his later years. His will left money to found a school of journalism at Columbia, and to establish the prizes that still carry his name. That was a genuine self-correction, made by the man who had helped create the problem. It does not erase what came before it, and it is worth reporting alongside.

One last thing, since this week's two primary sources are both by Theodore RooseveltTHEE-oh-dor ROH-zuh-veltA recurring figure across this course: Assistant Secretary of the Navy, Rough Rider, and later president, who led the volunteer cavalry charge at Kettle Hill in the Spanish-American War.. The skepticism this lesson asks of a Hearst headline applies to a president's memoir as well. A man writing about a charge he led has reasons of his own. Those reasons do not disappear because he was brave, and they do not disappear because he later held high office. Ask what he wants you to conclude, and then ask what his own words will actually support. Read him closely, and read him the same way.
