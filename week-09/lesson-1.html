📖 LESSON 9.1
American Empire: The Spanish-American War and the Acquisition of Hawaii and the Philippines
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Cuba had been fighting for independence from Spain on and off since 1868, and by the 1890s Spanish authorities were herding Cuban civilians into "reconcentration" camps to cut off support for rebel fighters, producing real suffering that American newspapers, as Tuesday's lesson examines, reported in vivid and not always accurate detail. When the American battleship USS Maine exploded in Havana harbor in February 1898, killing 266 sailors, the cause was almost certainly an accidental internal fire, but American public opinion, primed by months of press coverage, blamed Spanish sabotage. "Remember the Maine!" became the rallying cry for a war Congress declared two months later.

The war itself lasted barely four months and was, in Secretary of State John Hay's phrase, a "splendid little war" for the United States: the Spanish fleet was destroyed at Manila Bay and Santiago, American ground forces (including Roosevelt's volunteer cavalry regiment, Tuesday's subject) took Cuba, and Spain sued for peace by August. The Treaty of Paris that ended it transformed the United States into an overseas colonial power almost overnight: Spain ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States and gave up its claim to Cuba, which became formally independent but remained under heavy American influence through the Platt Amendment's restrictions on its foreign policy.

Hawaii's path to American control ran on a separate, earlier track. American sugar planters had overthrown Hawaii's Queen Liliuokalani in 1893 with the unofficial support of the U.S. minister to Hawaii and a landing party of Marines, then spent five years lobbying for annexation, achieved in July 1898 through a joint resolution of Congress rather than the two-thirds Senate treaty vote annexation would ordinarily require, since annexationists could not muster that supermajority. Both acquisitions provoked genuine domestic opposition: the Anti-Imperialist League, whose members included Mark Twain, argued that ruling foreign peoples without their consent betrayed the nation's own founding principles, a debate that would only sharpen once American forces began fighting a Philippine independence movement that had expected the war to end in its own freedom, not a change of colonial master.
