📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Cuba had been fighting Spain for independence, on and off, since 1868. The first long war ended in 1878 without independence. A new revolt broke out in 1895, and one of its causes came from Washington. An American tariff passed in 1894 shut Cuban sugar out of the market it depended on, and the island's economy collapsed. José Martíhoh-SAY mar-TEEThe Cuban independence leader who organized the 1895 revolt against Spain from exile before being killed in the fighting., the writer who had organized the movement from exile, was killed in the fighting that first year.

Spain sent General Valeriano Weylervah-lair-YAH-noh WY-lerThe Spanish general who forced rural Cubans into fortified towns, a brutal policy that shaped American opinion against Spain. to put the revolt down in 1896, and his method decided how Americans would see the war. Weyler ordered rural Cubans out of the countryside and into fortified towns, so the rebels would lose their food and their recruits. Very little provision was made for feeding the people once they arrived. Estimates of the dead run well into six figures. American newspapers reported the camps in vivid and not always accurate detail, as the next lesson examines.

Two weeks in February 1898 turned public anger into a war. On February 9, Hearst's New York Journal published what became known as the De Lome letterduh lohm LET-erA stolen private letter by Spain's minister to Washington calling President McKinley weak, published by Hearst's newspaper in 1898., a stolen private letter by Enrique Dupuy de Lome. De Lome was Spain's minister in Washington, and the letter called President McKinley weak and a chaser of crowds. He resigned within days. On February 15, the battleship Maineyoo-ess-ess maynThe American battleship that exploded in Havana harbor in February 1898, its unclear cause becoming a rallying cry for war., sent to Havana to protect Americans there, exploded at anchor. Two hundred sixty-six men died.

The cause has never been fully settled. A Navy court blamed an external mine within weeks, and a second board agreed in 1911. A study led by Admiral Hyman Rickover in 1974 reached a different conclusion. A fire in a coal bunker had most likely set off a forward magazine. That remains the best-supported explanation. None of it mattered in 1898. "Remember the Maine" was the cry, and Congress declared war in April. Congress also attached the Teller AmendmentTEL-er uh-MEND-mentA clause attached to the 1898 declaration of war disclaiming any U.S. intention of annexing Cuba., disclaiming any intention of annexing Cuba. That promise was kept, in form.

Now hold one detail up to the light. The war's most consequential battle was arranged before the war existed. Theodore RooseveltTHEE-oh-dor ROH-zuh-veltA recurring figure across this course: Assistant Secretary of the Navy, Rough Rider, and later president, who led the volunteer cavalry charge at Kettle Hill in the Spanish-American War. was Assistant Secretary of the Navy, not the Secretary. On February 25, 1898, with his superior out of the office for the afternoon, Roosevelt cabled Commodore George Deweyjorj DOO-eeThe U.S. Navy commodore who destroyed the Spanish fleet in Manila Bay in May 1898. at Hong Kong. He told Dewey to keep his squadron coaled, concentrated, and ready to attack the Spanish fleet in the Philippines the moment war came. His superior was annoyed the next day, but the orders stood. On the morning of May 1, Dewey destroyed the Spanish squadron in Manila Bay.

Ask what follows from that afternoon. Had the cable not gone out, the American Navy might not have been in the Philippines at all when the peace was written. There would have been no Philippine question, no Pacific empire to argue about, and a very different treaty. An empire is supposed to be the product of long design. This one turned partly on who was minding the office.

The war itself lasted barely four months. Secretary of State John Hayjahn hayThe U.S. secretary of state who called the Spanish-American War a "splendid little war." called it a splendid little war, and for the United States it was. The Spanish fleet in the Caribbean was destroyed off Santiago. On July 1, American troops took the heights above the city. Roosevelt's volunteer cavalry regiment, known as the Rough Ridersruf RY-derzTheodore Roosevelt's volunteer cavalry regiment, famous for its charge up Kettle Hill in Cuba. and the subject of this week's first primary source, charged Kettle Hill. It went up alongside the regular Army's Ninth and Tenth Cavalry. Those were Black regiments, and their part in the day drew far less newspaper attention than his own. Disease did most of the killing on the American side. Roughly 385 men died in battle, and about five times that many died of typhoid, yellow fever, and malaria. Week 14's second primary source, written by the Army doctor William GorgasWIL-yum GOR-gusAn Army doctor whose work fighting yellow fever and malaria is examined more fully in Week 14's Panama Canal lessons., takes up that problem directly.

The Treaty of ParisTREE-tee uv PAIR-issThe treaty ending the Spanish-American War, in which Spain ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines and gave up Cuba. was signed on December 10, 1898. Spain gave up its claim to Cuba and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines for twenty million dollars. Cuba became independent in form. In 1901 Congress attached the Platt Amendmentplat uh-MEND-mentA 1901 addition limiting Cuba's independence, reserving an American right to intervene there.. It limited Cuba's power to make treaties and to borrow money, reserved an American right to intervene, and secured a naval station. The Senate ratified the treaty on February 6, 1899, with one vote more than the two-thirds required. The United States became a colonial power by a single vote.

Hawaii ran on an earlier and separate track. American sugar planters overthrew Queen Liliuokalanilee-lee-oo-oh-kah-LAH-neeThe Hawaiian monarch overthrown by American sugar planters in 1893. in January 1893, with help from the American minister, who landed Marines from a warship in the harbor. President Cleveland had the affair investigated, concluded the overthrow was improper, and refused to send an annexation treaty to the Senate. For five years the annexationists could not find the two-thirds vote a treaty needs. What broke the deadlock was Manila Bay. A fleet in the Philippines needed a coaling station halfway across the Pacific, and suddenly the argument was strategic rather than commercial. In July 1898 Congress annexed Hawaii by joint resolution, which takes only a simple majority of both houses.

Then came the part the celebrations had not planned for. Emilio Aguinaldoeh-MEEL-yoh ah-gee-NAHL-dohThe Filipino independence leader whose forces fought American troops after expecting independence rather than new colonial rule.'s Filipino forces had fought Spain expecting independence, not a new governor. Fighting between them and the American army began in February 1899 and was declared over in 1902, though it continued in places well after that. About 4,200 American soldiers died. Filipino combatant deaths ran into the tens of thousands, and civilian deaths from combat, displacement, and disease into the hundreds of thousands.

Americans argued about all of this at the time, and the argument was not sentimental. The Anti-Imperialist Leaguean-tee-im-PEER-ee-uh-list leegAn organization including Mark Twain and Andrew Carnegie that opposed American colonial rule over the Philippines. included Mark Twain and Andrew Carnegie, who offered to buy the islands' independence with twenty million dollars of his own money. Their case was that governing a people who had no voice in their government contradicted the principle on which the United States claimed its own legitimacy. Beginning in 1901, the Supreme Court answered in a series of decisions. The new territories, it held, belonged to the United States without being fully part of it. The Constitution, in other words, did not follow the flag all the way.

Weigh the result the way this course has been weighing regimes. The new possessions enlarged American capacity to trade and to defend itself, which is a real accomplishment measured against a real problem. They also committed a republic to ruling people who could not vote for the government that ruled them, and left its army fighting former allies. Neither judgment cancels the other, and a student should be able to hold both. What is hardest to argue is that any of it was planned. A cable sent on a free afternoon. A treaty carried by one vote. A resolution used because a treaty could not pass. This is how the United States acquired an empire.
