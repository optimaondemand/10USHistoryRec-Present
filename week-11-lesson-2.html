📖 LESSON 11.3
The Haymarket Affair
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

On May 4, 1886, a rally in Chicago's Haymarket Square, called to protest police violence against striking workers at the McCormick Reaper Works the previous day, was drawing to a peaceful close when someone, whose identity has never been established with certainty, threw a bomb into a line of advancing police officers. Seven officers eventually died from the blast and the gunfire that followed; police shot into the crowd, killing an unknown number of civilians. In the panic that followed, Chicago police arrested eight anarchist labor organizers, not because any could be shown to have thrown the bomb, but because their fiery public rhetoric was judged to have inspired whoever did.

All eight were convicted; seven were sentenced to death, and four were ultimately executed in November 1887 (one killed himself in his cell; three later had their sentences commuted, and Illinois's governor pardoned the remaining three in 1893, concluding the trial had been unjust). The trial is now widely regarded, including by many historians at the time, as a serious miscarriage of justice: men were executed substantially for their political opinions rather than for any proven act.

Haymarket's consequences reached far beyond the eight men convicted. Public opinion, primed by newspaper coverage, fused the words "anarchist" and "labor agitator" into a single frightening category, discrediting the Knights of Labor (Week 7), which had no real connection to the bombing but suffered a collapse in membership regardless, and hardening the suspicion of foreign-born radicalism that runs through both of this week's primary sources. May 1 later became International Workers' Day in much of the world specifically in memory of the Haymarket martyrs, a global legacy largely unknown to most Americans today.
