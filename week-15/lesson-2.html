📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Yosemite's protected status predates the national park system itself by more than twenty years. In 1864, in the middle of the Civil War, Abraham Lincoln signed a bill called the Yosemite Grantyoh-SEM-ih-tee grantAn 1864 law signed by Lincoln setting aside Yosemite Valley and the Mariposa Grove for public use, the first time any government preserved scenic land for that purpose.. Congress had bigger concerns that year than a California valley. It still found time to act anyway. The bill ceded Yosemite Valley, along with the nearby Mariposa Grovemair-ih-POH-suh grohvA grove of giant sequoia trees protected alongside Yosemite Valley by the 1864 Yosemite Grant. of giant sequoias, to the state of California. California was to hold the land "for public use, resort, and recreation... inalienable for all time." It was the first time any government anywhere had set aside scenic land specifically for public preservation. No earlier grant like it had been made for economic development or military use instead. Senator John Connessjahn KAH-nessThe California senator who sponsored the Yosemite Grant bill. of California sponsored the bill. It passed with almost no debate at all, a small item of business tucked inside a much larger war.

It is worth pausing on what, exactly, Lincoln's signature protected. Yosemite Valley is walled in on both sides by sheer granite cliffs. Among them are El Capitan and Half Dome, formations rising thousands of feet almost straight up from the valley floor. Waterfalls drop from those cliffs each spring, fed by melting mountain snow, including one of the tallest waterfalls anywhere in North America. Early visitors often struggled to describe the place in ordinary language at all. Many reached instead for religious words like "cathedral" and "temple." Scenery like this is exactly what later moved Congress to act. It is what moved Roosevelt himself, a president not usually given to sentiment, to treat a landscape as something worth protecting for its own sake.

California appointed a local mountain guide named Galen ClarkGAY-len klarkThe mountain guide appointed as Yosemite Valley's first guardian under California's management. as the valley's first guardian. Clark had already spent years exploring the area on foot. He was the one who first measured and publicized the true size of the Mariposa Grove's giant sequoias. One tree, now called the Grizzly Giant, is more than two thousand years old. Its base is wider than a city bus is long. Even so, California's management of the grant proved inconsistent over the following decades. Grazing, logging, and casual tourist damage went largely unchecked. John Muirjahn myoorA self-taught naturalist who founded the Sierra Club and championed the preservation of wild land, including Yosemite.'s advocacy through the 1880s, described more fully in this week's first lesson, built the public case for stronger federal protection instead. In 1890, Congress created Yosemite National Parkyoh-SEM-ih-tee NASH-uh-nul parkA national park created by Congress in 1890 around Yosemite Valley, which California did not fully cede to federal control until 1906. around the valley. The valley itself was not initially included, oddly enough. California did not cede the valley and the Mariposa Grove back to federal control until 1906, sixteen years later.

The 1903 camping trip described in this week's first primary source activity happened in the middle of this same period, three years before that final federal consolidation. Roosevelt and Muir camped together for three nights in May 1903. Roosevelt deliberately left his usual security detail and press corps behind for the occasion. One night, the two men were caught in a sudden snowstorm at Glacier Point. They woke up under several inches of fresh snow. Muir used the isolation to press Roosevelt directly on the case for full federal control of the valley, a case Roosevelt would help see through to completion three years later. Robert Underwood Johnson, the Century editor from this week's first lesson, had been angling for exactly this kind of private meeting between Muir and Roosevelt for years beforehand.

The recession campaign that followed the trip was not automatic or easy. California's own legislature had to vote to give up land it had held for nearly forty years. The vote in Sacramento was close and contentious. Johnson's magazine campaign helped tip public opinion in Muir's favor. So did Muir's own personal lobbying of individual legislators, letter by letter. Not everyone in California favored recession, and the reasons for opposing it were not purely sentimental. Some timber and grazing interests preferred the state's looser oversight to federal management. A few San Francisco business leaders quietly worried that land held by the state might prove easier to lease for future water and power development than land locked inside a federal park. Their instinct was not wrong. Within a few years, the very question of using nearby Hetch Hetchyhech HECH-eeA Yosemite-area valley whose damming for San Francisco's water supply, opposed by Muir and supported by Pinchot, became the defining fight between preservationism and conservationism. Valley for the city's water supply, the fight this week's first lesson follows in full, would prove exactly how much was at stake in who controlled land like this. Congress finally accepted California's recession in 1906. The valley and the Mariposa Grove were folded into the national park at last, a single protected unit for the first time in Yosemite's history.

Whatever the 1903 camping trip's precise causal weight, alongside the many other factors shaping Roosevelt's conservation record, it stands as a genuine example of the Great Men framework this course generally favors. Two specific individuals met in a specific place, at a specific moment. That meeting plausibly mattered to an outcome that did not have to occur exactly when or how it did. Had Roosevelt never made that trip, California's recession vote might easily have gone differently. It might never have come up for a vote at all. Had the two men never found time alone together, away from Washington's usual pressures and Sacramento's usual politics, the whole sequence of persuasion the trip set in motion might never have started.

Yosemite today remains one of the most visited national parks in the country. It stands as a living monument to a debate that Muir and Pinchot never fully resolved between themselves: preservation against development, wilderness against use. This week's first lesson traced that same debate through the Hetch Hetchy controversy, a fight Muir lost in the very years Yosemite's own protection was finally being completed. The valley Lincoln protected almost as an afterthought during a war, and the valley Muir and Roosevelt walked together forty years later, turned out to be the same place. It was protected, in the end, by two very different kinds of decision, made two generations apart by people who never met each other: a wartime Congress acting almost in passing, and a lone naturalist who spent three cold nights convincing a president to finish what that Congress had only begun.
