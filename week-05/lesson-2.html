Andrew Carnegie and Pittsburgh: The Steel City

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Andrew CarnegieAN-droo kar-NAY-geeA Scottish immigrant who built Carnegie Steel through vertical integration and later gave away most of his fortune to public causes. arrived in America from Scotland as a poor thirteen-year-old and went to work in a cotton mill. He taught himself telegraphy, which was then a new and valuable skill, and took a job as a telegraph operator. That job put him in front of Thomas A. ScottTAH-mus ay skahtThe Pennsylvania Railroad executive who mentored young Andrew Carnegie and taught him to invest. of the Pennsylvania Railroad. Scott hired the young man as his personal assistant and became, in Carnegie's own telling, something close to a second father. He taught Carnegie two things that shaped everything afterward. The first was how a large railroad actually ran: divisions, schedules, and above all the cost sheet. The second was that a salary alone would never make a man rich. Scott steered him into his first investments, and Carnegie learned to make money while he slept. By his mid-thirties he had left the railroad to build things of his own, and in the early 1870s he chose steel.

The choice was not obvious at the time, which is what makes it interesting. Iron was the established material and steel was expensive and hard to make in quantity. On a trip to England in 1872, Carnegie saw the Bessemer converter in operation. The Bessemer process blew air through molten pig iron to burn out the impurities, turning a slow craft into a fast industrial one. Carnegie came home convinced that the price of steel was about to collapse. He also concluded that the man who built the biggest and most modern mill would own the market. He named that mill the Edgar Thomson WorksED-ger TAHM-sun werksCarnegie's first and most modern steel mill, opened in 1875 and named after his railroad mentor., after the president of the Pennsylvania Railroad, and opened it in 1875. The flattery was not accidental. Thomson's railroad was the largest single customer for steel rail in the country.

Carnegie built his empire through a strategy opposite to Rockefeller's. Rockefeller practiced horizontal integration, buying competing refineries at the same stage of production until he controlled the stage itself. Carnegie practiced vertical integration, owning every stage of the process instead. By the 1890s his Carnegie Steel Companykar-NAY-gee steel KUM-puh-neeAndrew Carnegie's vertically integrated steel company, sold to J. P. Morgan in 1901 to form U.S. Steel. owned iron ore ranges near Lake Superior. Some of them he leased from Rockefeller himself, in one of the era's stranger arrangements. He owned the ships that carried the ore across the Great Lakes and the railroads that hauled it inland. He owned the Connellsville coke that fueled the furnaces. He owned the mills that turned it all into rail and beam. Nothing his mills needed had to be bought from someone who could raise the price on him. A competitor who depended on the open market for even one of those stages could not match his costs.

The habit that made vertical integration pay was one Carnegie brought straight from the railroad: he measured everything. Most ironmasters of the era could tell you roughly what a season had cost. Carnegie wanted to know what each department had spent on each ton, this month against last month. "Watch the costs and the profits take care of themselves," he liked to say. He also built when everyone else was frightened. During the Panic of 1873, and again in the depression of the 1890s, contractors were desperate and materials were cheap. Carnegie expanded while his rivals cut back. That was nerve as much as arithmetic, and it worked. The price of steel rail fell by roughly nine tenths across his working life. Bridges, skyscrapers, and the streetcar rails of the previous lesson all became affordable because of it.

Carnegie's contemporary biographer Herbert Casson, writing in 1907, described him commanding this operation the way a general commands a battle. Carnegie directed strategy "from a near-by hill," Casson wrote, where he could survey the whole field, while trusting subordinates to run daily operations. Chief among those subordinates was Henry Clay FrickHEN-ree klay frikCarnegie's partner, the coke magnate who broke the union at the Homestead steel works in 1892., the coke magnate whose company Carnegie had absorbed. That trust cut both ways. In 1892, Frick moved to break the union at the Homestead works while Carnegie was on holiday in Scotland. Frick locked the men out, ringed the plant with fencing, and brought armed Pinkerton agents up the river by barge. The battle that followed left men dead on both sides, and the union in American steel was broken for the next forty years. This course returns to Homestead in Week 7. The question it leaves here is one about responsibility. How much blame does a man who delegates so completely bear for what his delegates do in his name? Historians still argue about it. The question grows harder when the delegate was chosen precisely because he would do it.

That question sharpens because of what Carnegie wrote. In 1889 he published the essay you will read later this week. A rich man, he argued, is merely a trustee of surplus wealth. He is bound in duty to administer it for the community. He meant it, and he acted on it. He gave away the great bulk of his fortune. It funded more than sixteen hundred public libraries in the United States alone, along with concert halls, universities, and a fund for international peace. Yet the wealth he distributed so thoughtfully was produced by men working twelve-hour shifts in heat that killed some of them. Those were the wages Frick was cutting when the lockout began. Carnegie never reconciled those two facts in public, and you should not assume the reconciliation is easy. Judging him well means holding both records in view at once.

The Pittsburgh he built all this in had been very different a generation earlier. By Casson's account it was a minor "importing and distributing point" with only seven small blast furnaces. Geography explains part of the transformation. Three rivers met there for cheap water transport, and the Connellsville coal fields lay close at hand. By the 1890s Pittsburgh was the undisputed steel capital of the world. Its skies were dark with furnace smoke around the clock, and its mills employed tens of thousands of men. In 1901 Carnegie sold the whole enterprise to J. P. Morgan for roughly 480 million dollars. Morgan folded it into United States Steelyoo-NY-ted stayts steelThe company J. P. Morgan formed in 1901 by buying Carnegie Steel, the first corporation worth more than a billion dollars., the first corporation in history worth more than a billion dollars. Carnegie spent the rest of his life giving the money away. The industrial transformation this unit has traced, from railroad to refinery to steel mill, ended for him in a library. For the men who made the steel, it ended somewhere else.
