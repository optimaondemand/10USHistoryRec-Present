📖 LESSON 5.2
Andrew Carnegie and Pittsburgh: The Steel City
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Andrew Carnegie arrived in America from Scotland as a poor thirteen-year-old and worked his way up through a telegraph office and the Pennsylvania Railroad before turning, in his mid-thirties, to steel. Where Rockefeller built his oil empire through horizontal integration, buying out competing refineries at the same stage of production, Carnegie built his steel empire through the opposite strategy: vertical integration. By the 1890s his Carnegie Steel Company owned the iron ore ranges near Lake Superior, the ships that carried the ore across the Great Lakes, the coke ovens that fueled the furnaces, the railroads that connected them, and the mills themselves, controlling every stage of production from raw ore to finished rail. No competitor who depended on the open market for any of those stages could match his costs.

Carnegie's contemporary biographer Herbert Casson, writing in 1907, described him managing this vast operation the way a general commands a battle: directing strategy "from a near-by hill" while trusting subordinates like Henry Clay Frick to run daily operations. That trust cut both ways. Frick's brutal handling of the 1892 Homestead Strike, which the course returns to in Week 7, happened while Carnegie was away in Scotland, and historians still debate how much responsibility a man who delegates so completely bears for what his delegates do in his name.

The Pittsburgh Carnegie built his empire in had been, by Casson's account, a minor "importing and distributing point" with only seven small blast furnaces a generation earlier. By the 1890s it was the undisputed steel capital of the world, its skies darkened by furnace smoke around the clock, its mills employing tens of thousands of workers whose lives, and whose 1892 strike, would come to represent the harshest edge of the industrial transformation this unit has traced from railroad to refinery to steel mill.
