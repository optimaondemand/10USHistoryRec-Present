📖 LESSON 5.1
Urbanization and America's Great Cities
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In 1860, no American city held a million people. By 1900, three did, and the country's urban population had roughly tripled. Industrial jobs pulled people off farms and off boats from Europe alike, concentrating them in cities built for a fraction of their new size. Chicago, New York, and Pittsburgh grew not by careful plan but by accretion, block by block, tenement by tenement, straining water systems, sewer systems, and fire departments designed for a much smaller town.

Two technologies, both drawing on the same steel this week's lesson will examine, made a new kind of city physically possible. Steel-frame construction let buildings rise past the five or six stories a load-bearing brick wall could support; Chicago's Home Insurance Building, completed in 1885, is often credited as the first true skyscraper. Electric streetcars, spreading rapidly after 1888, let a city's population commute rather than live within walking distance of a factory, and rings of streetcar suburbs began to grow around every major American city by the 1890s. Together, steel let cities grow up and streetcars let them grow out.

Neither technology solved the more basic problem of governing a city that had tripled in a generation. Municipal services could not keep pace with the demand, and into that gap stepped urban political machines, organizations like New York's Tammany Hall, which traded jobs, favors, and often outright bribes for votes, filling real gaps in immigrant newcomers' lives (a job, a Christmas basket, help with a legal problem) that no formal city government yet provided. Whether these machines were mainly corrupt exploiters of the poor or mainly informal welfare systems operating outside the law, or some mixture of both, is a question this course returns to directly in Week 11. For now, the essential fact is simpler: American cities in the Gilded Age grew faster than any institution, public or private, had a plan for managing.

2
