📖 LESSON 5.1
Urbanization and America's Great Cities
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In 1860, no American city held a million people. By 1900, three did. New York, Chicago, and Philadelphia had all crossed that line, and the country's urban population had roughly tripled in forty years. The proportion matters as much as the raw number. In 1860 about one American in five lived in a town or city. By 1900 it was close to two in five, and the country was moving toward a future in which most citizens would never farm. Two very different streams of people fed that growth. Industrial jobs pulled Americans off farms where machinery had made their labor unnecessary. The same jobs pulled millions more off ships from Europe. Both streams poured into cities built for a fraction of their new size. Chicago, New York, and Pittsburgh grew not by careful plan but by accretion, block by block and tenement by tenement. Water systems, sewer systems, and fire departments designed for a modest town were suddenly asked to serve a metropolis.

Chicago's case shows how contingent this growth could be. In October 1871 a fire burned through roughly three and a half square miles of the city and left about a third of its residents homeless. A disaster on that scale might have crippled a city permanently. Instead it cleared the ground in the commercial district. It also forced the city to write tougher building codes. Those codes pushed builders toward iron, steel, and masonry rather than wood. Architects and engineers arrived from across the country to take the work, and Chicago became the place where the tall building was invented. That outcome was not written into the fire. It depended on decisions the city made afterward. It also depended on luck. The railroads feeding Chicago's stockyards and grain elevators were untouched by the flames.

Three technologies made a new kind of city physically possible. The first was steel-frame construction. It let a building's weight rest on an internal metal skeleton rather than on its outer walls. A load-bearing brick wall tall enough to hold up ten stories would have to be so thick at street level that it swallowed the ground floor. A steel frame removed that limit. William Le Baron Jenney's Home Insurance Building, completed in Chicago in 1885, is often credited as the first true skyscraper. Cheap structural steel was the thing that made it buildable, and the next lesson takes up the man who made steel cheap. The second technology was the safety elevator. Elisha Otis had demonstrated a braking device that would catch a car if its cable snapped. Without that brake, no one would rent an office on a tenth floor. The third was the electric streetcar. Frank Sprague built the first large, reliable electric streetcar system in Richmond, Virginia, in 1888. Cities across the country copied it within a few years. Together, steel and elevators let cities grow up. Streetcars let them grow out. Rings of streetcar suburbs began forming around every major American city in the 1890s. For the first time, an ordinary clerk could live several miles from the office where he worked. A person's home no longer had to sit within walking distance of a job.

None of this solved the more basic problem of governing a city that had tripled in a generation. The difficulties were physical before they were political. Cities ran on horses. A large city housed well over a hundred thousand of them. Each produced manure by the ton every year, and some died where they stood in the street. Drinking water and sewage competed for the same rivers and lakes. Chicago poured its sewage into Lake Michigan. The city also drew its drinking water from the same lake. Typhoid killed people every year as a result. The city's eventual answer was extraordinary. In 1900 engineers reversed the flow of the Chicago River, so that waste ran away from the lake rather than into it. Tenement districts on New York's Lower East Side reached population densities among the highest ever recorded anywhere in the world. Fire, disease, and sheer crowding were not abstractions. They were daily facts, and Week 12 takes up the reform movement that finally attacked them.

Into the gap left by overwhelmed city government stepped the urban political machine. Organizations like New York's Tammany Hall traded jobs, favors, and often outright bribes for votes. To an immigrant family newly off a ship, the machine was frequently the only institution that answered. A ward boss could find a man work on a city crew. He could deliver coal in January, speak to a judge about a son in trouble, or put food on the table after a fire. No formal city agency yet did any of that. There was no unemployment office, no public relief worth the name, and no legal aid for the poor. What the boss asked in return was simple and reliable: a vote, and the votes of everyone in the household. The machines also stole on a scale that is genuinely hard to overstate. William M. Tweed and his associates took an amount from New York City that historians still estimate in the tens of millions of dollars. Much of it came through padded construction contracts on a single courthouse. Tweed's ring collapsed in the early 1870s under newspaper exposure and the cartoons of Thomas Nast. Nast drew Tweed so recognizably that the illustrations helped identify him after he fled the country.

The machine is worth judging carefully, because the easy answers in both directions are wrong. A later Tammany ward leader named George Washington Plunkitt defended himself in print with a memorable distinction. Dishonest graft, he said, meant outright theft. Honest graft meant learning in advance where the city planned to build, then quietly buying that land first. "I seen my opportunities and I took 'em," he said. Measured against the standard this course uses, the machine partly succeeded and fundamentally failed. It did resolve conflicts between citizens and did bind newcomers into a community that would otherwise have ignored them. But a government that works well administers common laws regularly and predictably. A citizen should know what to expect without having to know anyone. The machine did the opposite. It made access to public goods depend on personal loyalty to a particular man. That is a system a stranger cannot use and a reformer cannot fix from the outside. Week 11 returns to the conflict it eventually provoked. For now the essential fact is simpler. American cities in the Gilded Age grew faster than any institution, public or private, had a plan for managing.
