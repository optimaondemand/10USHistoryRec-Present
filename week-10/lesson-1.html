New Immigration: Southern and Eastern Europe Come to America's Cities

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Through most of the nineteenth century, most American immigrants came from Northern and Western Europe. They came from Ireland, Germany, Britain, and Scandinavia. Beginning in the 1880s, the pattern shifted sharply. Millions of Italians began arriving. So did Jews from the Russian Empire and Austria-Hungary, Poles, Greeks, and other Southern and Eastern Europeans. Contemporaries called this the "New Immigrationnoo im-ih-GRAY-shunThe wave of immigrants arriving mainly from Southern and Eastern Europe after 1880, distinct from earlier immigration from Northern and Western Europe.." Between 1880 and 1920, roughly twenty million immigrants entered the United States. By 1907, close to eighty percent came from Southern and Eastern Europe rather than the older sending countries. Roughly four million of the total were Italian. Roughly two million were Jews from the Russian Empire alone.

Most passed through one building: Ellis IslandEL-iss EYE-lundThe immigration inspection station in New York Harbor that processed most European immigrants beginning in 1892., in New York Harbor. It opened in 1892. It replaced an earlier station called Castle Garden. On its busiest recorded day, April 17, 1907, inspectors processed almost twelve thousand people. A steerageSTEER-ijThe cheapest class of travel on a steamship, located below deck, used by most immigrants crossing the Atlantic. ticket, the cheapest class below deck, cost around thirty dollars. The crossing itself had once taken weeks or months under sail. Steam power cut it to a week or two. That material change made migration at this scale possible in the first place. Steerage itself was cramped and often unsanitary, packed with bunks below the waterline, far from the cabins where wealthier travelers rode. Disease could spread quickly in those close quarters, and inspectors at Ellis Island watched closely for it, since a contagious illness could mean a return voyage at the steamship line's own expense.

Push and pull factors worked together, as they had for the German farmers of Week 2's Great Plains. In the Russian Empire, laws restricted where Jews could live and work. Waves of violent attacks called pogromspoh-GRAHMAn organized, often government-tolerated violent attack on a Jewish community, common in the Russian Empire in this era., like the one at Kishinev in 1903, killed and terrorized Jewish communities directly. In southern Italy, a region called the Mezzogiornomed-zoh-JOR-nohThe Italian name for the country's poor, largely agricultural southern half, the region most New Immigration-era Italian immigrants left., rural poverty and small divided landholdings pushed many families to leave. Disease outbreaks pushed still more. On the American side, industrial jobs pulled newcomers in. So did letters and money sent home by relatives already settled, and aggressive recruiting by steamship companies eager to fill westbound berths.

Unlike the Homestead Act generation, most New Immigrants settled in cities rather than on farms. They filled factory, construction, and garment-industry jobs. They clustered in dense ethnic neighborhoods: Chicago's Little Italy, New York's Lower East Side. There a newcomer could find a shared language, a familiar church or synagogue, and countrymen who might help find work. New York's garment trade, concentrated on the Lower East Side, grew heavily dependent on Jewish immigrant labor in these same decades. That work shaped the neighborhood the same way steel shaped Pittsburgh in Week 5.

These newcomers looked, prayed, and sounded different from the mostly Protestant, English-speaking immigrants of earlier decades. That difference fed a nativist backlash. It grew steadily louder over the following decades. The American Protective Associationuh-MAIR-ih-kun proh-TEK-tiv uh-soh-see-AY-shunA nativist organization founded in 1887 that opposed the New Immigration., founded in 1887, and the Immigration Restriction League, founded in 1894, argued that the New Immigrants carried crime, radical politics, and a racial inability to become "real" Americans. Congress created a study, the Dillingham CommissionDIL-ing-um kuh-MISH-unA congressional commission (1907 to 1911) that studied immigration and produced racially biased conclusions, though some of its narrower factual findings held up., to try to document those claims scientifically. It worked from 1907 to 1911 and produced forty-one volumes of findings. Historians today regard the commission's racial conclusions as unsupported prejudice dressed in the language of research. It was not settled fact. The anthropologist Franz Boasfrants BOH-azAn anthropologist whose research for the Dillingham Commission found that immigrant children's physical measurements changed within a generation, undermining the commission's fixed racial theories., working for the same commission, ran his own study of immigrant children's skull shapes. He found the measurements changed within a single generation raised in America, evidence against the fixed racial categories the rest of the report assumed. His findings did not stop the report's final conclusions from leaning the other way. That is an important caution about mistaking official-looking data for objective truth.

That backlash did not turn into law immediately. Nothing about it was inevitable. Congress passed a bill requiring immigrants to pass a literacy test three separate times. Three separate presidents vetoed it: Grover Cleveland in 1897, William Howard Taft in 1913, and Woodrow Wilson in 1915. Only in 1917 did Congress finally override a presidential veto and enact the literacy requirement. That fact is worth pausing on. For two full decades, the executive branch alone stood between the restrictionist argument and the law. Then it did not. This week's second primary source, the Immigration Act of 1924im-ih-GRAY-shun akt uv NYN-teen TWEN-tee-forA law replacing the literacy test with strict national quotas calculated from the 1890 census, deliberately favoring immigrants from Britain and Germany., completed what the literacy test had only begun. It replaced the test with strict national quotas, one for each country of origin. Those quotas were deliberately calculated from the 1890 census, not the more recent 1910 census. The choice was not neutral. It counted the country's population from before most of the New Immigration had even arrived, guaranteeing small quotas for Italy and the Russian Empire and large ones for Britain and Germany. This time no veto stood in the way.

A foreign-language press grew up alongside these communities. Yiddish newspapers, Italian-language papers, and Polish-language papers gave newcomers local news, job listings, and advice in a language they already read. Some, like the Yiddish daily ForvertsFOR-vertsYiddish for "Forward." A widely read Yiddish-language daily newspaper founded in New York in 1897, serving Jewish immigrant readers., founded in 1897, reached readerships in the hundreds of thousands within a generation. These papers helped a newcomer navigate an unfamiliar city long before he learned enough English to read an American one.

One irony is worth naming here. The Statue of Liberty's pedestal was funded partly by a newspaper fundraising campaign. Joseph Pulitzer ran that campaign, described in Week 9. The pedestal now carries Emma Lazarus's 1883 sonnet. It welcomes the "huddled masses yearning to breathe free." Those words were written, and eventually mounted, years before Congress moved to keep most of those same masses out.

New Immigrants did not all share a single fate once they arrived. Chances differed by port of entry and by region of origin. Immigrants arriving at Angel Island in San Francisco Bay, most of them from Asia, faced far harsher screening and much longer detention than those who passed through Ellis Island, a difference this course's supplementary material examines directly. The story told in this week's lessons is the Ellis Island story, a European one. It is only part of a larger picture.
