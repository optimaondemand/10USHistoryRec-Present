📖 LESSON 10.3
Bensonhurst: Brooklyn's Little Italy
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Bensonhurst, a neighborhood on Brooklyn's southern edge, grew from farmland into one of New York's largest Italian-American communities within roughly two decades of the New Immigration's arrival. Its growth followed a pattern historians call chain migration: one man from a Sicilian or Neapolitan village would arrive, find work and lodging, and then send for a brother, then a cousin, then eventually enough of an entire village's population that Bensonhurst's blocks came to mirror the geography of specific Italian towns thousands of miles away, an old-country map redrawn onto Brooklyn streets.

Work often arrived through the padrone system: an Italian-speaking labor broker who arranged jobs, housing, and sometimes loans for newly arrived countrymen, charging fees that made him, depending on the individual padrone's honesty, either an indispensable guide through an unfamiliar system or an exploiter skimming a vulnerable newcomer's wages. Mutual aid societies, often organized by hometown or region, provided sick benefits, funeral costs, and a social hall where the old language and old customs could be maintained, functioning as an informal insurance system for people the formal American economy offered none.

The sharpest tension in a neighborhood like Bensonhurst ran between generations rather than between Italians and native-born Americans directly. Immigrant parents who had crossed an ocean to keep their language, religion, and customs intact watched their American-schooled children speak English at home, adopt American dress and slang, and sometimes marry outside the community altogether, a case study, at the scale of individual families, in exactly the "Americanization" process the previous primary source both demanded and, from the other side of it, many immigrant families experienced as a genuine loss.
