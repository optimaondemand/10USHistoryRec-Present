Bensonhurst: Brooklyn's Little Italy

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Bensonhurst sits on Brooklyn's southern edge. It was once farmland. Within roughly two decades of the New Immigration described in the previous lesson, it became one of New York's largest Italian-American communities. New elevated and trolley lines helped make that possible. They pushed steadily into Brooklyn's outer neighborhoods in these same years. Cheaper land opened up at the city's edge, just as demand for housing exploded.

Bensonhurst's growth followed a pattern historians call chain migrationchayn my-GRAY-shunA pattern in which one immigrant's arrival leads to family members and neighbors following over time, often from the same home village.. One man from a Sicilian or Neapolitan village would arrive first. He would find work and lodging. Then he would send for a brother, then a cousin. Eventually much of an entire village followed. Bensonhurst's blocks came to mirror the geography of specific Italian towns thousands of miles away. It was an old-country map, redrawn onto Brooklyn streets.

Most of these arrivals were contadinikohn-tah-DEE-neeItalian for "peasant farmers." Landless farm laborers, the class most Italian immigrants of this era came from., peasant farm laborers from the Mezzogiornomed-zoh-JOR-nohThe Italian name for the country's poor, largely agricultural southern half, the region most New Immigration-era Italian immigrants left. described in the previous lesson. A striking number never intended to stay permanently. Historians estimate that as many as half of all Italian immigrants in this period eventually returned to Italy. Many went home after several years of American wages. They earned the nickname "birds of passageberdz uv PASS-ijA nickname for immigrants, especially Italians, who worked seasonally in America and regularly returned home rather than settling permanently.." A man might cross the Atlantic more than once. He might work a construction season in Brooklyn, then a planting season back home. In between, he sent remittances, money sent home to family. This pattern is invisible in a simple immigrant head count. It meant Bensonhurst's population was never quite as settled, or as permanent, as it looked from the outside.

Work often arrived through the padronepah-DROH-nayItalian for "boss" or "patron." A labor broker who found immigrant workers jobs and housing in exchange for a fee, sometimes fairly and sometimes exploitatively. system. A padrone was an Italian-speaking labor broker. He arranged jobs, housing, and sometimes loans for newly arrived countrymen. He charged fees for all of it. Depending on the padrone's honesty, he was either an indispensable guide or an exploiter skimming a vulnerable newcomer's wages. Congress tried to restrict the worst of this system with the Foran Act of 1885FOR-an akt uv AY-teen AY-tee-fyvA federal law banning employers from contracting foreign labor in advance, aimed at curbing padrone-system abuses.. That law banned employers from contracting foreign labor in advance. The padrone system still continued informally for decades afterward. It ran on personal relationships and cash, not the kind of written contract a law could easily reach. The same Dillingham Commission described in the previous lesson investigated padrone abuses directly. On this narrow question, its findings held up far better than its racial conclusions did. Real exploitation existed alongside real prejudice. A careful student learns to judge each claim on its own evidence, not to dismiss every conclusion a flawed source reached.

Mutual aid societiesMYOO-choo-ul ayd suh-SY-uh-teezImmigrant community organizations that provided sick benefits, funeral costs, and social support in place of formal insurance. filled the gap the padrone system left. They were often organized by hometown or home region. They provided sick benefits and funeral costs. They kept a social hall where the old language and old customs survived. They worked as an informal insurance system, for people the formal American economy offered none. Patron-saint feast days served a similar purpose in a different form. Villages brought these celebrations over from home. They marked them with processions through Bensonhurst's streets. A whole neighborhood publicly renewed its attachment to a village most of its younger members had never actually seen. Many households also took in boarders. These were often unmarried male relatives or fellow villagers, renting a bed or a corner of a room. The extra income helped a family cover rent that one wage earner's pay alone could not.

Walking down a Bensonhurst block around 1910, a visitor would have heard Italian dialects before English. Pushcart peddlers sold vegetables, fish, and imported cheese along the curb. Small grocery stores stocked olive oil and dried pasta that a general American grocer would not have carried. These businesses were not a sideline. They were often a family's first foothold into ownership, a step up from day labor. A shop counter, unlike a factory floor, was a place where a newcomer's own language was an asset rather than a barrier.

Church life carried its own quiet conflict. Italian immigrants were overwhelmingly Catholic. But the American Catholic hierarchy they encountered was largely built and run by earlier Irish immigrants and their children. Italian parishioners sometimes felt like guests in a church that was, in name, their own. Many neighborhoods eventually gained a national parish. That was a congregation organized around a specific immigrant language and its own patron saints, rather than a fixed geographic boundary. It gave Italian Catholics a religious home that finally felt like theirs.

The sharpest tension in a neighborhood like Bensonhurst, though, ran between generations. It did not run mainly between Italians and native-born Americans. New York State's compulsory schooling laws put immigrant children in classrooms taught in English. Settlement houses like the ones described in Week 8 ran evening "Americanization" classes for the adults. Immigrant parents had crossed an ocean to keep their language, religion, and customs intact. They watched their American-schooled children speak English at home. They watched them adopt American dress and slang. Some children even married outside the community altogether. Children old enough to read English, but not yet old enough to vote, often became their own parents' interpreters. They read official letters. They spoke for the family at school, or at a landlord's office. It was a reversal of the ordinary authority between parent and child. Girls in particular often left school earlier than their brothers. Many families expected daughters to contribute wages well before adulthood, through factory work or piecework sewing done at home. All of this was a case study, at the scale of individual families, in the exact "Americanization" process the previous primary source demanded. Many immigrant families, from the other side of that process, experienced it as a genuine loss.

None of this made Bensonhurst, or neighborhoods like it, a simple story. It was not assimilation simply succeeding or failing on some fixed schedule. The chain migration that built the neighborhood, the padrone system that both helped and exploited newcomers, and the birds of passage who never meant to stay, all show something else. A community was solving practical problems for itself, with the tools at hand. It was not marching toward some predetermined American identity on a fixed timetable.
