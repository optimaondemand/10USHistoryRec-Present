📖 TODAY'S LESSON

A trickle of Black Southerners had already begun moving north in these decades. This came before the larger wartime wave the course will examine later. They were escaping a legal system of racial segregation, known collectively as Jim Crow lawsjim kroh lawzSouthern state laws enforcing racial segregation in public accommodations, transportation, and schools.. Southern state legislatures built that system steadily after federal Reconstruction oversight ended in 1877, described in Week 1. These laws segregated railroad cars, schools, and public accommodations. Alongside them came poll taxes and literacy tests. Those tests stripped most Black men of voting rights the 14th and 15th Amendments had guaranteed on paper. That was part of the Nadir this course examined in Week 8. Sharecropping debt made the pressure worse. So did the constant threat of racial violence. Together, these forces pushed some families toward the promise of industrial wages in cities like Chicago, Detroit, and New York. It was the earliest current of what would become known as the Great Migrationgrayt my-GRAY-shunThe movement of Black Southerners to Northern cities, beginning as a small trickle in this era and growing much larger during and after World War I.. Their arrival added a further layer to cities already straining, as Week 5 described, under unmanaged growth. It often met hostility, not only from white residents, but from earlier immigrant communities competing for the same entry-level industrial jobs.

This early current of the Great Migration was still small next to what came later. It mattered anyway. Every family that moved sent word back home, in letters and in visits. Word of an available job, or a rooming house that took Black tenants, traveled through those same personal networks that carried Italian and Jewish newcomers into their own neighborhoods. A path that a later, much larger migration would follow was being built one household at a time.

The Washington-Du Bois disagreement was, in its own way, the same question this week's other conflicts were asking. It was just posed to a different excluded group. How should someone shut out of full citizenship respond? Should they work patiently within the system as it stands? Should they build whatever ground can be gained without direct confrontation? Or should they reject the system's terms outright, and demand full inclusion now? This course applies a Classical Republican standard elsewhere, and it applies here too. Both men were trying to solve the same first-order political problem. That problem was a political community's failure to recognize some of its own members as full citizens. Reasonable people, then and now, disagree about which approach did more to actually solve it.

Those same crowded industrial neighborhoods were filled with New Immigrants. Many came from societies with their own long traditions of radical politics. These neighborhoods became fertile ground for socialist and anarchist organizing, largely absent from earlier American labor movements. Socialists argued for socialismSOH-shuh-liz-umA political and economic theory holding that workers should collectively own the means of production.: the idea that workers should collectively own the means of production, through the state or through worker cooperatives. Eugene V. Debs was one leading voice. He had been radicalized in prison after the 1894 Pullman boycott, described in Week 7. In 1901 he founded the Socialist Party of AmericaSOH-shuh-list PAR-tee uv uh-MAIR-ih-kuhA political party founded by Eugene Debs in 1901.. He ran for president five separate times and never won. But he drew a genuine, lasting share of the vote, roughly six percent at his high point in 1912.

Anarchists held to anarchismAN-ar-kiz-umA political theory holding that the state itself is the fundamental obstacle to human freedom and should be abolished., a much smaller movement than socialism but a far more sensationally covered one. Its adherents argued that the state itself was the fundamental obstacle to human freedom and should be abolished outright. Emma GoldmanEM-uh GOHLD-munA leading American anarchist voice of this era. and Alexander Berkmanal-ig-ZAN-der BERK-munAn anarchist who attempted to assassinate Henry Clay Frick during the 1892 Homestead strike. were the movement's best-known American voices. In 1892, during the Homestead strike described in Week 5, Berkman shot and stabbed Henry Clay Frick in an assassination attempt. Frick survived, fighting back fiercely. Berkman spent fourteen years in prison for it. American public opinion, as this week's primary sources show, often treated socialists and anarchists as interchangeable. It treated both as foreign imports, incompatible with American life. That was not quite fair. Homegrown American radical traditions, including the author of this week's second primary source, predated most of the New Immigration entirely.

Urban political machines, introduced in Week 5, offered a third competing answer to the same disorder that fed radical politics. Their answer was not revolution, but patronage. Favors were traded for votes, within the existing system rather than against it. New York's Tammany Hall was the era's most famous machine. Its ward boss, George Washington Plunkitt, already introduced in Week 5, worked this exact trade daily. A coal delivery in winter. A job for a nephew. A lawyer's help after an arrest. All of it in exchange for a family's loyal vote on election day. A machine boss and a socialist organizer were, in this sense, answering the identical question. How does a poor immigrant family get help in a city that offers almost none officially? Their prescriptions were opposite. One said work within the corrupt system as it exists. The other said tear it down and build something else. Washington's and Du Bois's disagreement belongs on the same spectrum. It was a version of the identical underlying question, asked by a community facing exclusion of a different kind, for different historical reasons, in these same crowded decades.
