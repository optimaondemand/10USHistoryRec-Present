The Haymarket Affair

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The eight-hour movement had been building for years before the Haymarket AffairHAY-mar-ket uh-FAIRA May 1886 Chicago labor rally at which a bomb killed police officers, leading to the conviction of eight anarchists despite no proof any of them threw it. happened. A federation of craft unions, the ancestor of the American Federation of Labor described in Week 7, called for a nationwide general strike. It set the date for May 1, 1886. The demand was simple: an eight-hour workday for every American worker. Roughly three hundred thousand workers walked out that day. Tens of thousands walked out in Chicago alone, a city that had become one of the movement's strongest centers. Two days later, on May 3, police fired on strikers outside the McCormick Reaper Works. Several strikers died. Chicago's anarchist organizers called a protest rally for the following evening. It was held in Haymarket Square, to answer that violence.

On May 4, 1886, the rally was drawing to a peaceful close. Then someone threw a bomb into a line of advancing police officers. That person's identity has never been established with certainty. Some historians have pointed to a man named Rudolph Schnaubelt, who fled the city and was never caught. The evidence was never strong enough to call this settled, though. Seven officers eventually died from the blast and the gunfire that followed. Police shot into the crowd, killing an unknown number of civilians.

That group was associated with a militant editor named Johann Most. Chicago's radicals called their own creed the "Chicago Ideathuh shih-KAH-goh eye-DEE-uhA strain of anarchism holding that unions, not any government, should organize society once the state was abolished.." It was a strain of anarchism holding that unions themselves, not any government, should organize society once the state was abolished. In the panic after the bombing, Chicago police arrested eight men connected to that movement. Their names were Spies, Albert Parsons, Samuel Fielden, Michael Schwab, George Engel, Adolph Fischer, Louis Lingg, and Oscar Neebe. None could be shown to have thrown the bomb. The prosecution never seriously argued otherwise. Instead, the state argued something different: their fiery public speeches and writings had inspired whoever did. It was a legal theory that punished opinion rather than any proven act.

The trial itself ran for roughly two months in the summer of 1886. A special bailiff hand-selected the jury pool, rather than drawing it by the usual random process. The presiding judge, Joseph Gary, allowed it. Later observers widely regarded Gary as openly hostile to the defense throughout the proceedings. All eight men were convicted. Seven were sentenced to death. Lingg took his own life in his cell the night before the scheduled execution. He used a smuggled dynamite cap. Four others were hanged on November 11, 1887: Spies, Parsons, Engel, and Fischer. Fielden's and Schwab's sentences had already been commuted to life in prison. Illinois's governor at the time, Richard J. Oglesby, made that decision the day before the executions. He gave no public reason for sparing those two men and not the others, which only deepened the sense that the sentences had been arbitrary rather than carefully judged.

One detail complicates the "foreign radicalism" story this episode fed. Most of the eight defendants were German immigrants, and newspapers played that fact hard. But Albert Parsons was born in Alabama, a native-born American with roots reaching back to the colonial era. He could have fled after the bombing, as several other suspects did, but he turned himself in for trial instead, believing his American birth and his innocence would protect him. It did not. His execution alongside seven immigrant codefendants was, on its own, evidence that the crowd's fear was broader than any one nationality.

In 1893, Illinois's new governor, John Peter Altgeldjahn PEE-ter AWLT-geldThe Illinois governor who pardoned the three surviving Haymarket defendants in 1893, stating the trial had been unjust., pardoned the three men still living: Fielden, Schwab, and Neebe. He stated publicly that the trial had been unjust. The men had been convicted for their beliefs, he said, rather than for any crime the state could prove. The pardon was politically costly. Altgeld's career never fully recovered from the backlash it caused. It was a real price paid for what most historians now consider a genuinely courageous act.

Judge this trial by the standard this course applies elsewhere. A political community has a duty to provide lawful means of resolving conflict between citizens. By that standard, Haymarket looks like a clear failure. It satisfied a public demand for someone to blame. It did not establish what had actually happened. Contingency runs through the whole episode, too. Had police not advanced on a rally that witnesses described as already breaking up peacefully, there might have been no confrontation at all. Had the bomb-thrower's identity ever been established, the trial of eight other men might never have happened in the first place.

Haymarket also shaped the labor movement's own strategy. Samuel Gompers's American Federation of Labor, described in Week 7, deliberately kept its distance from radical politics after Haymarket. Gompers focused narrowly on wages, hours, and working conditions, avoiding the broader political and revolutionary claims the Knights of Labor and the anarchists had both made in their different ways. That caution helped the AFL survive and grow for decades, long after the Knights collapsed, though it also meant leaving the deeper political questions Debs and the anarchists raised largely unanswered.

Haymarket's consequences reached far beyond the eight men convicted. Public opinion, primed by newspaper coverage, fused the words "anarchist" and "labor agitator" into a single frightening category. It discredited the Knights of Labor (Week 7). That organization had no real connection to the bombing, but it suffered a collapse in membership regardless. Haymarket hardened the suspicion of foreign-born radicalism that runs through both of this week's primary sources. In 1889, delegates from socialist parties around the world met in Paris. They founded the Second International there. That same congress declared May 1, May Daymay dayMay 1, adopted internationally as a workers' holiday in 1889 in memory of the Haymarket defendants., an international workers' holiday, specifically in memory of the Haymarket defendants. That choice turned the date of the original eight-hour strike into a permanent global commemoration. It is still marked in much of the world today. Most Americans do not know this history. They instead mark Labor Day in September.
