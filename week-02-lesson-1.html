📖 LESSON 2.1
The West & Agrarian America
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The Homestead Act of 1862 offered any citizen, or immigrant intending to become one, 160 acres of public land free of charge, provided they lived on it and improved it for five years. On paper, it was an invitation to a nation of small farmers. In practice, it collided with a fact Congress had not solved by legislation: the Great Plains already belonged, in every sense that mattered on the ground, to the horse-mounted nations who lived there. The Sioux, Cheyenne, Comanche, and their neighbors had spent more than a century mastering a mobile, buffalo-based way of life that let them strike, trade, and vanish across hundreds of miles of open grassland. No Homestead Act could be made real until that power was broken, and breaking it took the United States Army the better part of three decades, several humiliating defeats (Custer's destruction at the Little Bighorn in 1876 chief among them), and a deliberate campaign against the Plains tribes' economic base: the buffalo herds themselves, hunted toward extinction by the 1880s.

Technology did as much as policy to decide the outcome. The repeating rifle and, later, railroads that could move troops and settlers alike gave the Army and the homesteaders who followed it an advantage no earlier generation of settlers had possessed. Joseph Glidden's barbed wire, patented in 1874, let a farmer with no timber for a fence enclose 160 acres cheaply, ending the era of open, unfenced range almost as soon as it had properly begun. German, Scandinavian, and Bohemian immigrants poured onto the plains behind the railroads, drawn by cheap land and free passage; whole counties in Nebraska and the Dakotas took on the language and church customs of Central Europe within a single generation.

Between the buffalo's fall and the farmer's fence lay one more economy, brief but unforgettable: the open-range cattle drive. Texas longhorns, descended from Spanish stock and multiplying wild for decades, were worth a few dollars a head in Texas and forty dollars a head at a railhead in Kansas. Cowboys, many of them Black or Mexican veterans of the cattle trade, drove herds of a thousand or more up trails like the Chisholm to meet the railroad, a decade-long boom that barbed wire and a catastrophic winter in 1886-87 brought to a fairly sudden end. None of it, from the Army's slow campaigns to the cowboy's brief golden age, proceeded on any fixed schedule. It was a contest for the same ground between two ways of life, decided as much by weather, weapons, and railroads as by any law passed in Washington.
