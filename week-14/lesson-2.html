Roosevelt's Foreign Policy: The Panama Canal
📖 LESSON 14.3
Building of the Panama Canal
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The Panama Canal was not the first attempt. A French company under Ferdinand de Lesseps, fresh from building the Suez Canal, began digging across Panama in 1881 and abandoned the project eight years later, financially ruined and having lost an estimated twenty thousand workers, mostly to yellow fever and malaria, diseases whose actual cause, transmission by a specific mosquito species, was not yet understood by French engineers who assumed the danger came from foul air or poor sanitation in the ordinary sense.

When the United States took over construction in 1904, following the events described in Monday's lesson, American engineers inherited the same disease environment that had defeated the French. The crucial difference was that Walter Reed's Army medical team had proven in 1900, through deliberate experiments in Cuba, that yellow fever was transmitted specifically by the female Aedes aegypti (then called stegomyia) mosquito, not by general uncleanliness. That discovery meant the disease could be attacked at its actual source rather than fought in the abstract, and it fell to Thursday's primary source author to prove the theory could be applied at the scale of an entire construction zone.

Only once yellow fever and malaria were brought under control could the canal's actual engineering proceed: cutting through the Continental Divide at Culebra, building the largest dam and largest artificial lake in the world at the time to create Gatun Lake, and designing a lock system to raise and lower ships eighty-five feet between sea level and the lake. The canal opened in August 1914, a decade after American work began, a project that succeeded where the French had failed not primarily because of better engineering, but because of a medical breakthrough that made a hostile disease environment survivable for a large workforce.
