📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The Panama Canal was not the first attempt to build one. A French company under Ferdinand de LessepsFER-dih-nand duh leh-SEPSThe French engineer who built the Suez Canal and led the failed French attempt to build the Panama Canal., fresh from his success building the Suez Canalsoo-EZ kuh-NALA sea-level canal in Egypt built by Ferdinand de Lesseps, whose design he unsuccessfully tried to copy in Panama., began digging across Panama in 1881. De Lesseps tried to copy his Suez design almost exactly: a canal cut straight through at sea level, with no locks at all. Panama was not Egypt, though. Suez had been flat desert. Panama was mountainous jungle instead, cut through by the flood-prone Chagres River. A sea-level canal there would have required moving far more earth than anyone had actually planned for.

The company abandoned the project eight years later, financially ruined. It had lost an estimated twenty thousand workers, mostly to yellow fever and malaria. French engineers did not understand what actually caused those diseases. They assumed the danger came from foul air or ordinary poor sanitation, so they never even suspected the real cause. The company's collapse also set off a massive corruption scandal back in France. Investigators found that company officials had bribed dozens of French politicians and journalists to keep selling worthless stock to the public. Some officials went to prison. For years afterward, the French used the very word "Panama" as shorthand for a corruption scandal.

When the United States took over construction in 1904, following the events described in this week's first lesson, American engineers inherited the exact same disease environment that had defeated the French. This time, though, the disease question had already been solved. A Cuban physician named Carlos FinlayKAR-lohss FEEN-lyThe Cuban physician who first proposed in 1881 that a specific mosquito, not foul air, spread yellow fever. had proposed back in 1881 that a specific mosquito, not foul air, spread yellow fever. Nobody believed him for two decades. Then, in 1900, an Army medical team led by Walter ReedWAWL-ter reedThe Army doctor who led the 1900 team that confirmed Finlay's mosquito theory of yellow fever transmission. tested Finlay's idea directly in Cuba. Two of Reed's own researchers, James Carroll and Jesse Lazear, let infected mosquitoes bite them on purpose to prove the theory. Carroll survived a severe case. Lazear did not; he died of the yellow fever his own experiment gave him. His death proved Finlay right.

Reed's findings meant yellow fever could finally be attacked at its actual source. Colonel William GorgasWIL-yum GOR-gusThe doctor whose mosquito-control campaign made it possible for a large workforce to survive building the Panama Canal., this week's second primary source author, took that discovery and applied it at a scale no one had tried before. Gorgas had already used the same methods to nearly eliminate yellow fever in Havana. In Panama, his teams fumigated buildings and screened windows and water barrels. They also drained or oiled standing water across the entire canal zone, denying mosquitoes anywhere to breed at all. Yellow fever cases in Panama fell to zero by 1906. Malaria took longer to control but dropped sharply as well. Without Gorgas's work, a large workforce could not have survived long enough to finish the canal at all.

Only once the disease problem was brought under control could the canal's actual engineering proceed. Even then, the sea-level design the French had tried was still not the right answer for Panama's terrain. John Stevensjahn STEE-venzThe American chief engineer who chose a lock canal design over a sea-level canal and built essential infrastructure before large-scale digging began., the American chief engineer appointed in 1905, made that judgment early and pushed for a very different plan: a lock canal, raising ships up over the mountains rather than cutting straight through them. Stevens also insisted on building working railroads, decent housing, and real sanitation for the workforce first. Only after that groundwork was laid did he allow large-scale digging to begin. It was a patient approach, and it frustrated an impatient Roosevelt, but it later proved essential. Stevens resigned suddenly in 1907, for reasons he never fully explained. Roosevelt replaced him with an Army engineer, George Washington Goethalsjorj WAH-shing-tun GOH-thulzThe Army engineer who took over and completed the Panama Canal after Stevens resigned., who saw the project through to its finish.

More than fifty thousand workers labored on the canal at its peak, most of them recruited from Barbados and other West Indian islands. Roosevelt himself traveled to Panama in 1906 to inspect the work firsthand, the first time a sitting American president had ever left the country's own territory while in office. Photographs of Roosevelt at the controls of a giant steam shovel became some of the most widely printed images of his entire presidency.

The achievement carried a real human cost that deserves an honest accounting, not just a celebration. Roughly fifty-six hundred workers died during the American construction period, most from accident and disease. That was a small fraction of the French toll, but it was still a heavy price to pay. The workforce itself was strictly divided by a system workers called the "gold rollgohld rohl / SIL-ver rohlThe segregated pay and housing system on the Panama Canal, paying white American workers in gold with better conditions and other laborers in silver with worse conditions." and the "silver roll." American workers, almost all of them white, were paid in gold. They received better housing, better food, and access to whites-only clubhouses. West Indian and other non-American laborers did most of the heaviest and most dangerous digging. They were paid in silver, at lower wages, and kept in separate, poorer facilities of their own. The canal's completion is rightly remembered as an engineering triumph. It is also a clear example of the same racial hierarchy this course has traced through the New South and through American immigration, applied here to a workforce building American infrastructure on foreign soil.

With the workforce and the disease problem both under control, the digging itself could finally proceed at full scale. Workers cut through the Continental Divide itself at a pass called Culebrakoo-LAY-bruh kutThe section of the Panama Canal cut through the Continental Divide, requiring the removal of massive amounts of rock and soil.. Steam shovels removed well over one hundred million cubic yards of rock and soil there, and landslides kept sliding fresh material back into the cut almost as fast as crews could dig it out. Builders dammed the Chagres River to create Gatun Lake. At the time, it was the largest artificial lake anywhere in the world. A system of locks then raised and lowered ships eighty-five feet, carrying them between sea level and that lake. The canal finally opened in August 1914, a full decade after American work began. Its opening was almost immediately overshadowed by a different, far larger event: that same August, war broke out across Europe, the war this course studies in Week 17. The canal succeeded where the French had failed. The real difference was not superior engineering alone. It was a medical breakthrough that made a hostile disease environment survivable for a very large workforce. It also took an engineer patient enough to build the supporting systems before ever touching the mountain.
