📖 LESSON 14.1
Big Stick Diplomacy and the Making of Panama
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Roosevelt summarized his foreign policy with a proverb: "speak softly and carry a big stick." In practice, this meant maintaining enough military strength, especially naval strength, that the United States rarely needed to use force to get its way in the Western Hemisphere. The clearest test of the doctrine came over a canal across the Isthmus of Panama, then a province of Colombia. When Colombia's Senate rejected a proposed treaty granting the United States canal rights in 1903, on terms Colombia judged too favorable to the Americans, Roosevelt's administration made little secret of its displeasure, and when a Panamanian independence movement rose up weeks later with covert encouragement and the conspicuous presence of American warships preventing Colombian troops from landing to suppress it, the United States recognized the new Republic of Panama within days and signed a canal treaty on far more favorable terms than Colombia had rejected.

Roosevelt never denied his administration's role, later boasting privately that he "took the Isthmus," though he insisted publicly that Panama's revolution was a genuine popular movement the United States had simply not obstructed. Critics at the time and since have called the episode a thinly disguised act of American-engineered regime change; defenders note that Panama's population had long resented rule from Bogotá and that Colombia's own rejection of a reasonable treaty precipitated the crisis. Neither position fully resolves the contingency at the heart of the episode: had Colombia's Senate simply ratified the original 1903 treaty, there would likely have been no Panamanian revolution and no controversy to debate at all.

Roosevelt's successor William Howard Taft pursued a related but distinct approach nicknamed "Dollar Diplomacy," using American financial investment and loans, rather than military intervention or the implicit threat of it, to extend American influence in Latin America and Asia. Where Roosevelt's stick was naval power, Taft's was economic leverage, a difference of method more than of underlying goal, since both presidents shared the conviction that the United States had both the right and the responsibility to shape events within its hemisphere.
