📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Roosevelt summarized his foreign policy with a proverb: "speak softly and carry a big stick." In practice, this meant keeping enough military strength, especially naval strength, to get its way in the Western Hemisphere. The United States rarely needed to actually use that force. Just having it was usually enough. The clearest test of the doctrine came over a canal across the Isthmus of PanamaIS-mus uv PAN-uh-mahThe narrow strip of land connecting North and South America where the Panama Canal was built., then a province of Colombia.

The need for that canal had already been proven in the war this course studied back in Week 9. When the battleship USS Oregonyoo-ess-ess OR-uh-gunA battleship whose two-month voyage around South America in 1898 demonstrated the strategic need for a Central American canal. was ordered from the Pacific coast to join the fleet fighting Spain in 1898, it had to sail all the way around South America, a voyage of more than two months. The war very nearly ended before the Oregon arrived. Roosevelt, who had pushed hard for a strong navy as assistant secretary of the Navy before that war even began, never forgot the lesson. A canal across Central America could turn that two-month voyage into a matter of weeks. The Great White Fleet, sixteen battleships Roosevelt sent on a world tour beginning in 1907, painted white rather than the navy's usual gray, was his way of showing every nation that American naval power could now reach any ocean on short notice. A shorter route between the oceans was the missing piece.

The canal itself was not a new idea. American engineers had studied two possible routes for decades: one through Panama, one through Nicaragua. A federal commission actually favored the Nicaragua route as late as 1902. Panama's French concession still demanded a steep payment, which made Nicaragua look cheaper on paper. The final choice tipped toward Panama anyway, for reasons that had almost nothing to do with engineering. A French lobbyist named Philippe Bunau-Varillafih-LEEP boo-noh-vah-REE-yahA French lobbyist for the failed French canal company who helped finance and organize Panama's 1903 independence movement. represented the failed French canal company. He wanted Congress to buy his company's rights before they expired worthless. Bunau-Varilla mailed every senator a Nicaraguan postage stamp showing a smoking volcano. It was a none-too-subtle reminder that the Nicaragua route ran near an active volcanic range. The Senate chose Panama, by a margin of eight votes.

Securing Panama meant securing Colombia's consent first, since Panama was still Colombian territory in 1903. American diplomats negotiated the Hay-Herrán Treaty with Colombia's foreign minister, Tomás Herrán. It offered ten million dollars, plus annual rent, for canal rights. Colombia's Senate rejected the treaty unanimously. Colombian senators judged the American payment too small. They also had a separate reason to simply wait. The French company's concession was set to expire in 1904. Once it expired, Colombia itself might collect the roughly forty million dollars the United States had already promised to pay the French company instead.

Roosevelt's administration made little secret of its fury at the rejection. Weeks later, a Panamanian independence movement rose up. It was led by a local doctor named Manuel Amador Guerrero. Bunau-Varilla still hoped to save his company's payment, so he helped finance the plot. He drafted a constitution for the new republic in advance. He even supplied a flag, reportedly sewn by his own wife. The warship USS Nashville arrived off the Panamanian coast just before the uprising began. American forces then blocked Colombian troops from landing to put the revolt down. The revolution succeeded with almost no bloodshed. One account claims only a Chinese shopkeeper and a donkey died in the fighting. The United States recognized the new Republic of Panamarih-PUB-lik uv PAN-uh-mahThe nation created in 1903 after a revolution against Colombia, which the United States quickly recognized and negotiated canal rights with. within days.

Panama then made Bunau-Varilla its minister to Washington, a Frenchman who had never once set foot in the country. He negotiated the Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treatyhay boo-noh-vah-REE-yah TREE-teeThe 1903 treaty granting the United States a ten-mile-wide Canal Zone "in perpetuity." before Panama's own delegates could even arrive to object to its terms. It granted the United States a ten-mile-wide Canal Zone "in perpetuity." Those terms were considerably more favorable than the treaty Colombia had just rejected. Roosevelt never denied his administration's role in any of it. He later boasted privately that he "took the Isthmus." Publicly, though, he insisted Panama's revolution was a genuine popular movement, one the United States had simply not obstructed.

Critics at the time and since have called the whole episode a thinly disguised act of American-engineered regime change. Defenders see it differently. They note that Panama's population had long resented rule from distant Bogotá. They also note that Colombia's own rejection of a reasonable treaty is what precipitated the crisis in the first place. Neither position fully resolves the contingency sitting at the center of this story. Had Colombia's Senate simply ratified the original 1903 treaty, there would likely have been no Panamanian revolution at all. There would be nothing here to argue about. A handful of Colombian votes, cast for reasons of price and timing rather than principle, ended up changing the shape of a map.

The following year, Roosevelt announced what became known as the Roosevelt CorollaryROH-zuh-velt KOR-uh-lair-eeAn addition to the Monroe Doctrine asserting a U.S. right to intervene in Latin American nations to prevent European intervention over unpaid debts. to the Monroe Doctrinemun-ROH DOK-trinAn 1823 U.S. policy statement opposing European colonization or intervention in the Western Hemisphere.. It asserted a right for the United States to intervene in Latin American nations whose financial disorder might otherwise invite European intervention instead. Roosevelt applied the Corollary almost immediately. The Dominican Republic owed enormous debts to European creditors in 1905. Roosevelt worried a European power might land troops to collect them. Germany, Britain, and Italy had briefly blockaded Venezuela over unpaid debts just two years earlier, and Roosevelt did not want that story repeated closer to American shores. Rather than let it happen again, American officials simply took over the Dominican customs house themselves. They collected the import duties directly and forwarded an agreed share to the country's foreign creditors. It was Big Stick diplomacybig stik dih-PLOH-muh-seeTheodore Roosevelt's foreign policy approach of maintaining strong military power, especially naval strength, to get results without necessarily using force. applied to bookkeeping instead of warships. The arrangement became the model for several later American interventions across the Caribbean and Central America. Roosevelt's successor, William Howard Taft, pursued a related but distinct approach nicknamed "Dollar DiplomacyDAH-ler dih-PLOH-muh-seePresident Taft's foreign policy approach favoring American financial investment and loans over military intervention.." Taft preferred American financial investment and loans as his main tool of influence. Military intervention, or even the threat of it, was his last resort, at least in theory. The method did not always stay purely financial in practice. American-backed loans to Nicaragua's government collapsed into default in 1912. Taft still sent Marines ashore to protect American interests there anyway, exactly the kind of intervention Dollar Diplomacy was supposed to make unnecessary. Where Roosevelt's stick was naval power, Taft's was economic leverage, a real difference of method on the surface. Underneath, both presidents shared the same conviction. The United States, they both believed, had both the right and the responsibility to shape events within its own hemisphere.
