Closing of the West: Conservation
📖 LESSON 1
Conservationism, John Muir, and the Closing of the Frontier
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

In 1890, the U.S. Census Bureau announced that it could no longer draw a meaningful line on the map marking a continuous American "frontier," the settled land Week 2 traced from the Homestead Act had finally filled in. Historian Frederick Jackson Turner argued three years later that this frontier, the ongoing process of settling open land, had shaped American character itself, fostering individualism and democracy in ways that would need some new outlet once the frontier closed for good. Whether or not Turner's specific thesis holds up, the practical fact he was responding to was real: the West that Week 2 opened with settlers, railroads, and cattle drives was, by century's end, a West with no more open land left to claim.

That closing produced a genuine and lasting anxiety, worth taking seriously as its own historical force, that America's most spectacular natural places might be destroyed by the same logging, mining, and development that had transformed the rest of the continent. John Muir, a Scottish-born naturalist who had wandered California's Sierra Nevada for years, founded the Sierra Club in 1892 specifically to advocate for total, untouched preservation of wild land, a philosophy historians now call "preservationism." Gifford Pinchot, the first head of the U.S. Forest Service and Roosevelt's close conservation advisor, held a related but genuinely different view, sometimes called "conservationism" or "wise use": that natural resources should be scientifically managed for sustained, long-term use, logging forests on a renewable cycle rather than either exhausting them or locking them away entirely.

Muir and Pinchot, close allies for years, split openly over San Francisco's proposal to dam Hetch Hetchy Valley, a granite valley Muir considered nearly as magnificent as Yosemite itself, for a municipal reservoir; Pinchot supported the dam as a legitimate "wise use" of a natural resource for a growing city's water supply, while Muir fought it as the destruction of an irreplaceable wild place. The dam was ultimately built in 1913, a defeat for Muir's preservationist vision, but the argument itself, still unresolved in American environmental politics today, is the direct ancestor of the wilderness-versus-managed-use debates that continue over public land to this day.
