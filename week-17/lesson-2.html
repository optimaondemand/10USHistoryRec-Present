📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Historians call the five weeks between June 28 and early August 1914 the July Crisisjoo-LY KRY-sissThe five-week period between Franz Ferdinand's assassination and the outbreak of general war in Europe in summer 1914.. The assassination itself very nearly did not happen. Earlier that same morning, a different conspirator named Nedeljko Čabrinović threw a bomb at Franz FerdinandARCH-dook frants FER-dih-nandThe heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne whose assassination in June 1914 triggered the events leading to World War I.'s car. The bomb bounced off the folded convertible top and exploded behind the vehicle, wounding people in a following car instead. Franz Ferdinand's motorcade continued its planned schedule anyway. Later that day, the archduke insisted on visiting the officers wounded in the morning's attack. His driver, unfamiliar with a last-minute change of route, made a wrong turn onto a side street. The driver then stopped the car to reverse, directly in front of a delicatessen where a second conspirator, nineteen-year-old Gavrilo PrincipGAV-rih-loh PRIN-sipThe nineteen-year-old Serbian nationalist who assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand., happened to be standing. Princip stepped forward and fired the shots that killed both Franz Ferdinand and his wife, Sophie. Sophie herself was riding along that day only because of the couple's own long fight against the Habsburg court. She came from minor nobility, not the royal rank the court demanded for an archduke's wife. Franz Ferdinand had married her anyway, only after years of resistance from his own family. A different route, a different driver, or a canceled hospital visit could each, on their own, have meant no assassination at all.

The five weeks that followed moved with grim speed once set in motion. Austria-Hungary delivered its ultimatum to Serbia on July 23, giving Belgrade just forty-eight hours to respond. Serbia's reply on July 25 accepted nearly every demand, but not quite all of them. Austria-Hungary's own leadership judged that partial acceptance a rejection. Vienna declared war on Serbia and shelled Belgrade that same day, July 28. Vienna moved too quickly for the Kaiser's own last-minute doubts, described below, to reach it in time to matter. Russia ordered general mobilizationmoh-bih-lih-ZAY-shunThe process of organizing and deploying a country's armed forces and supplies for war. on July 30 to defend its fellow Slavic nation. Germany issued ultimatums of its own to both Russia and France on July 31, demanding each halt its preparations. When neither backed down, Germany declared war on Russia on August 1 and on France on August 3. German troops crossed into Belgium on August 4, carrying out the Schlieffen PlanSHLEE-fen planGermany's war plan calling for a rapid strike through Belgium to defeat France before Russia could fully mobilize. from the previous lesson. Britain declared war on Germany that same day, over Belgium's violated neutrality. Ten days, from ultimatum to a continent-wide war.

No single document captures what followed better than this week's second primary source. It is a set of telegrams exchanged directly between Kaiser Wilhelm IIKY-zer VIL-helm thuh SEK-undThe German emperor whose "blank check" assurance to Austria-Hungary helped set the July Crisis in motion. of Germany and Tsar Nicholas IIzar NIK-uh-luss thuh SEK-undThe Russian emperor whose telegram exchanges with Kaiser Wilhelm II failed to prevent war in 1914. of Russia. The two monarchs were first cousins who addressed each other by family nicknames, "Willy" and "Nicky." Between July 29 and August 1, they traded urgent personal appeals. Nicholas warned of an "ignoble war" and asked Wilhelm to restrain Austria-Hungary. Wilhelm urged Nicholas to halt Russia's mobilization. Both men, by their own words, wanted to prevent exactly the war that followed. Nicholas's July 29 telegram pleaded plainly: "I foresee that very soon I shall be overwhelmed by the pressure brought upon me and forced to take extreme measures which will lead to war." Wilhelm, for his part, had already shown real second thoughts the day before. On July 28, the Kaiser read Serbia's conciliatory reply to the ultimatum for the first time. He scrawled a note in its margin, calling it "a great moral victory for Vienna." That victory, he added, removed "every pretext for war." Neither man was lying to the other. Each genuinely believed, at some point in these five days, that peace was still possible.

Their telegrams failed to stop anything. The reason why is itself the crisis's central lesson. By the time Wilhelm and Nicholas were pleading with each other directly, each nation's military had already begun specific, timetabled mobilization plans. Planners had built these vast logistical schedules over the preceding decade. They moved millions of men and tons of supplies by rail on a fixed calendar. Germany's own military chief, Helmuth von MoltkeHEL-moot fahn MOLT-kuhGermany's military chief, who warned that mobilization, once begun, could not be safely stopped., warned the Kaiser about that calendar directly. Once mobilization began, Moltke argued, it could not be safely reversed or even paused. Stopping partway would leave a nation catastrophically exposed. An enemy that kept mobilizing would gain every advantage a pause surrendered. Two men with the personal authority to declare war or peace found that the machinery beneath them had already acquired its own momentum. Their goodwill could not arrest it.

Not every historian accepts that account without qualification. The historian Christopher ClarkKRIS-tuh-fer klarkA historian whose 2012 book The Sleepwalkers argued European leaders retained more real choice in 1914 than the "unstoppable machinery" account suggests. made this case most prominently, in his influential 2012 study The Sleepwalkers. Clark argues that European leaders retained more real choice than the "unstoppable machinery" story suggests. Individual decisions, not just fixed timetables, deserve real blame in his account. This course does not need to resolve that scholarly disagreement to draw a lesson from it. Both the machinery and the choices were real. Historians still argue over exactly how much weight each deserves, and that argument is itself a healthy sign of honest scholarship rather than a problem to be embarrassed by.

This is contingency and structural constraint operating together, not against each other. Contingency, because the war did not have to start. It took this specific assassination, at this specific moment, with these specific alliance commitments already in place. Even the assassination itself hung on a wrong turn down a Sarajevo side street. Structural constraint, because once those alliances and mobilization plans existed, the crisis narrowed everyone's real choices fast. The range of options actually available to individual leaders shrank drastically within days, however powerful those leaders were on paper. Both truths belong in a complete account of how the July Crisis became the First World War.

Judged in Classical Republican terms, the crisis exposed a genuine first-order political failure shared by every great power involved. A political community succeeds partly by maintaining the capacity to deliberate carefully before committing itself to war. None of Europe's governments, for all their diplomatic experience, managed that in the summer of 1914. This week's first lesson already showed how one still-neutral power, the United States, watched that failure unfold from across the Atlantic and tried, for nearly three years, to avoid repeating it. Week 18 picks up the story at the moment that effort finally ran out.
