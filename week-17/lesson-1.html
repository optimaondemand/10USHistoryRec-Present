📖 LESSON 17.1
World War I: Outbreak, American Neutrality, and Preparedness
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Europe in 1914 was organized into two rival alliance blocs. Germany and Austria-Hungary anchored one bloc, called the Central Powers. France and Russia anchored the other, with Britain drawing closer to both over the preceding decade. Historians call that second bloc the Triple Entente. Italy technically belonged to a third alliance, alongside Germany and Austria-Hungary. But Italy stayed neutral in 1914. It switched sides entirely the following year. That switch is a useful reminder. Even the alliance system was not as fixed as it looked on paper. These blocs grew from a web of mutual defense commitments. Diplomats built that web over decades. They meant it to deter exactly the kind of war it ultimately guaranteed.

A Serbian nationalist assassinated the Austro-Hungarian heir, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914. Franz Ferdinand himself had actually favored more autonomy for Austria-Hungary's Slavic subjects, a policy that might have eased the tensions his own death helped ignite. Germany's Kaiser Wilhelm II responded on July 5 with what historians call the "blank check." It was a private assurance to Austria-Hungary's leaders. Germany would back them no matter how harshly they punished Serbia. Backed by that promise, Austria-Hungary issued Serbia an ultimatum. Most historians judge its terms deliberately close to unacceptable. Serbia rejected part of it. That partial rejection triggered Austria-Hungary's declaration of war. Russia then began mobilizing troops in Serbia's defense. Germany answered with its own long-standing war plan, called the Schlieffen Plan. That plan assumed a two-front war against both France and Russia was inevitable once general mobilization began. It called for a rapid strike through neutral Belgium, meant to knock France out of the war quickly. Germany's planners hoped to finish France off before Russia's much larger, slower army could fully mobilize in the east. Germany declared war on Russia, then on France, then invaded Belgium to carry out that plan. Belgium's neutrality was itself protected by an 1839 treaty. Britain declared war on Germany once German troops crossed the Belgian border. In barely five weeks, a single assassination in a provincial capital had become a war involving every major European power.

President Wilson declared American neutrality on August 4, 1914. He urged Americans to be "impartial in thought as well as in action," a standard many found genuinely difficult to meet. Millions of German-Americans and Irish-Americans, the latter hostile to Britain over its rule of Ireland, felt little natural sympathy for the Allied cause. Cultural and economic ties still pulled the country's policy hard toward the Allies. British and French loans and trade soon dwarfed German trade, especially once Britain's naval blockade cut most direct American commerce with Germany. American economic interest tilted toward the Allied side almost from the war's first months, whatever Wilson's official policy still said.

Both sides worked hard to shape American opinion, not just American trade. Britain controlled the transatlantic telegraph cables, giving London's version of events a real head start over Berlin's in reaching American newspapers. A British government inquiry, the 1915 Bryce Report, publicized detailed accounts of German atrocities during the invasion of Belgium. Some of those accounts were accurate; others were later shown to be exaggerated or invented outright. At the time, most American readers had no easy way to tell the difference, and the report did lasting damage to German credibility in the United States. Germany ran its own, far smaller information campaign from its embassy in Washington, but it never matched Britain's reach into the American press.

Two specific incidents strained that neutrality without yet breaking it. In May 1915, a German submarine sank the British liner Lusitania, killing nearly twelve hundred people, including 128 Americans. Wilson answered with a series of three increasingly firm diplomatic protest notes, rather than a declaration of war. Week 16 examines that choice directly, through Bryan's resignation over the second of those three notes. In March 1916, a German submarine torpedoed the French passenger ship Sussex, wounding several Americans aboard. Wilson's protest that time won an actual concession. Germany issued the Sussex pledge, a promise to stop sinking merchant ships without warning. That pledge held only until early 1917, as Week 18 will show.

Not every American accepted neutrality as sufficient in the meantime. A Preparedness Movement emerged, with Theodore Roosevelt as its most prominent voice. Retired General Leonard Wood organized volunteer officer-training camps at Plattsburgh, New York, starting in 1915, drawing thousands of young men who paid their own way to attend. Roosevelt argued that the United States needed a dramatically expanded army and navy right away. This was true, in his view, regardless of whether or when the country actually entered the war. Belligerent powers were already ignoring smaller neutral nations' rights, he warned. Only real military strength would earn America a hearing at any future peace table. Wilson resisted large-scale preparedness through most of 1915 and campaigned for re-election in 1916 on the slogan "He kept us out of war." He did eventually sign the National Defense Act of 1916, a real but partial compromise that enlarged the Army and brought state National Guard units more fully under federal control. That same year, Congress also passed a major Naval Act, funding new battleships aimed at building, within a few years, a navy "second to none" in the world. Even Wilson, cautious as he was about the Army, judged naval strength too important to leave underfunded.

Judged in Classical Republican terms, each man had a genuine point. Roosevelt's decisiveness matched Hegel's own test of a state's health: a state's capacity to prepare for and meet an external threat when it arrives. Wilson's caution reflected real prudence, a refusal to let war fever dictate policy before policy truly required it. Neither man was simply right and the other simply wrong. Their disagreement was genuine and consequential, and it played out inside the same first-order political problem every nation in this course has faced: whether its institutions and habits could actually mobilize in time to meet a threat, once one arrived. It would matter a great deal to the shape of Week 18's story.
