World War I: Outbreak, American Neutrality, and Preparedness
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Europe in 1914 was organized into two rival alliance blocs, Germany and Austria-Hungary on one side, France and Russia (with Britain drawing closer to both) on the other, bound by a web of mutual defense commitments built up over decades to deter exactly the kind of war they ultimately guaranteed. When a Serbian nationalist assassinated the Austro-Hungarian heir Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, Austria-Hungary issued Serbia an ultimatum harsh enough to be, by most historians' judgment, deliberately close to unacceptable. Serbia's partial rejection triggered Austria-Hungary's declaration of war, which triggered Russia's mobilization in Serbia's defense, which triggered Germany's declaration of war on Russia and then France under its own alliance obligations, which brought Britain in when Germany invaded neutral Belgium to reach France. In barely five weeks, a single assassination in a provincial capital had become a war involving every major European power.

President Wilson declared American neutrality on August 4, 1914, and urged Americans to be "impartial in thought as well as in action," a standard many found difficult given cultural and economic ties: British and French loans and trade dwarfed German trade once the British naval blockade took hold, tilting American economic interest, however officially neutral American policy remained, toward the Allied side almost from the war's first months.

Not every American accepted neutrality as sufficient. A Preparedness Movement, with Theodore Roosevelt as its most prominent voice, argued that the United States, regardless of whether or when it entered the war, needed a dramatically expanded army and navy simply to defend its own interests and be taken seriously by belligerent powers already ignoring smaller neutral nations' rights. Wilson himself resisted large-scale preparedness through 1915, wary of provoking exactly the war fever Roosevelt seemed eager to stoke, a genuine and consequential disagreement between two men whose different judgments would matter a great deal to the shape of Week 18's story.
