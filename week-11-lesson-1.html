📖 LESSON 11.1
Urban Conflict: Great Migration, Anarchism, Socialism, and Political Machines
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Even before the larger wartime wave the course will examine later, a trickle of Black Southerners had already begun moving north in these decades, escaping a legal system of racial segregation, known collectively as Jim Crow laws, that Southern state legislatures built steadily after federal Reconstruction oversight ended in 1877: laws segregating railroad cars, schools, and public accommodations, and, alongside them, poll taxes and literacy tests designed specifically to strip most Black men of the voting rights the 14th and 15th Amendments (Week 1) had guaranteed on paper. These laws, combined with sharecropping debt and the constant threat of racial violence, pushed some families toward the promise of industrial wages in cities like Chicago, Detroit, and New York, the earliest current of what would become known as the Great Migration. Their arrival added a further layer to cities already straining, as Week 5 described, under unmanaged growth, and often met hostility not only from white residents but from earlier immigrant communities competing for the same entry-level industrial jobs.

Those same crowded industrial neighborhoods, filled with New Immigrants from societies with their own long traditions of radical politics, became fertile ground for socialist and anarchist organizing largely absent from earlier American labor movements. Socialists argued that workers should collectively own the means of production through the state or worker cooperatives; anarchists, a much smaller but more sensationally covered movement, argued that the state itself was the fundamental obstacle to human freedom and should be abolished outright. American public opinion, as this week's primary sources show, often treated the two as interchangeable and treated both as foreign imports incompatible with American life, even though homegrown American radical traditions, including Wednesday's primary source author, predated most of the New Immigration entirely.

Urban political machines, introduced in Week 5, offered one competing answer to the same disorder that fed radical politics: not revolution, but patronage, favors traded for votes within the existing system rather than against it. A machine boss and a socialist organizer were, in this sense, answering the identical question, how does a poor immigrant family get help in a city that offers almost none officially, with opposite prescriptions: work within the corrupt system as it exists, or tear it down and build something else.
