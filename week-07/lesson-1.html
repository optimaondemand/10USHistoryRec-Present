📖 LESSON 7.1
Robber Barons, Muckrakers, Unions, and Strikes
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By the 1880s, critics had a name for the men whose fortunes this unit has traced: robber barons, a term meant to evoke medieval lords who taxed river traffic by force rather than earned their wealth by trade. The label fit some careers better than others, as Weeks 3 through 5 have already shown, but it captured a real and growing public sense that industrial power had outrun any check on how it was used, against competitors, against consumers, and above all against the workers whose labor produced the wealth.

Labor's response took organized form more than once, and more than once it ended in violence. The Great Railroad Strike of 1877, sparked by wage cuts during a depression, spread across a dozen states and required federal troops to suppress, the first time since the Civil War the U.S. Army had been used domestically on this scale. The Knights of Labor, briefly the largest labor organization in the country, tried to unite skilled and unskilled workers of every trade into one body, but collapsed after being unfairly blamed for the Haymarket bombing (covered fully in Week 11). Its place was taken by Samuel Gompers's American Federation of Labor, which pursued a narrower but more durable strategy: organizing skilled workers by craft and bargaining for concrete gains, wages, hours, working conditions, rather than the Knights' broader vision of remaking the economy altogether. In 1892, Carnegie's own Homestead steel plant became a battlefield when manager Henry Clay Frick, defending the company against a strike while Carnegie stayed safely in Scotland, hired private Pinkerton guards who fought an open gun battle with strikers, leaving several men dead on both sides.

Alongside organized labor, a new kind of journalist emerged, one willing to spend months or years documenting a single industry's abuses in exhaustive, difficult-to-dispute detail rather than a single dramatic exposé. President Roosevelt, who found some of this reporting excessive, gave the movement its lasting, half-critical name in a 1906 speech, comparing its practitioners to the "man with the muck-rake" in Pilgrim's Progress, so intent on raking filth that he could not look up to see a celestial crown offered above him. Whatever Roosevelt's reservations, muckraking journalism produced some of the most consequential reporting of the era, including the work of the next lesson.

🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain how the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, the Knights of Labor, the American Federation of Labor, and the Homestead Strike each represented a different response by organized labor to industrial power
2
I will be able to describe how muckraking journalism, named by Roosevelt's own half-critical 1906 label, changed how the public learned about abuses of industrial power
