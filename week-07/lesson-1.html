📖 LESSON 7.1
Robber Barons, Muckrakers, Unions, and Strikes
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

By the 1880s, critics had a name for the men whose fortunes this unit has traced. They called them robber barons. The phrase reached back to medieval lords who taxed river traffic by force rather than earning wealth by trade. It appeared in print against Vanderbilt as early as 1859. A historian's 1934 book later fixed it as the standard label for the whole generation.

The name fit some careers better than others, as Weeks 3 through 5 have shown. Carnegie built mills that made steel cheaper for everyone who bought a rail or a bridge. Gould made much of his money moving paper rather than freight. Still, the label caught something real. Industrial power had outrun any check on how it was used. It was used against competitors, against consumers, and above all against the workers whose labor produced the wealth.

Labor's answer took organized form more than once, and more than once it ended in gunfire. In July 1877, the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad cut wages for the third time in three years. Firemen and brakemen at Martinsburg, West Virginia, refused to let the freight trains move. The strike ran down the rail lines within days. It reached Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Chicago, and St. Louis. Strikers and city crowds burned the Pennsylvania Railroad's roundhouse in Pittsburgh. State militia fired into crowds in several cities. Roughly a hundred people died before the strike broke. President Hayes then sent federal troops to reopen the lines.

That order is worth pausing over. Hayes had withdrawn the last federal troops from South Carolina and Louisiana in April of the same year, ending Reconstruction. Week 1's second lesson described the bargain behind that withdrawal. In July he sent soldiers against American strikers. The Army was available for one internal conflict and not for the other. Nothing in the Constitution required that choice.

The strike exposed a deeper problem. A political order can be judged by whether it gives citizens a lawful way to settle quarrels among themselves. In 1877 the United States had almost no such machinery for the largest quarrel in its economy. There were no labor boards, no bargaining rules, and no legal standing for a union contract. So a wage dispute became a street battle, and a street battle became a military deployment. Much of what follows this week, and in Weeks 12 and 13, is the story of Americans trying to build the machinery that was missing.

Notice which lesson the states drew instead. In the years after 1877, legislatures across the North reorganized their militias and built stone armories in the middle of major cities. Some of those buildings still stand. They were designed with civil disorder in view, which meant they were designed with strikes in view. The immediate answer to a breakdown of order was to prepare for the next breakdown, not to prevent it.

The Knights of Labor tried to build it from below. Uriah Stephens founded the order in 1869, and Terence Powderly led it after 1879. The Knights invited nearly everyone: skilled workers and unskilled, women, and in many local assemblies Black workers as well. A won strike against Jay Gould's southwestern railroads in 1885 made the order famous, and membership reached roughly 700,000 within a year. Then a bomb went off at a labor rally in Chicago's Haymarket Square in May 1886. The Knights had not organized the rally, but the public blamed them, and the order fell apart within a few years. Week 11 takes up Haymarket in full.

Samuel Gompers drew the opposite lesson. His American Federation of Labor, also organized in 1886, gave up the goal of remaking the economy. It organized skilled workers by craft and bargained for what could be written into a contract: wages, hours, and conditions. Gompers called this pure and simple unionism. It proved far more durable than the Knights' broad vision. It was also far narrower. The AFL largely left out the unskilled immigrants filling the mills, and it did little for Black or women workers.

Two strikes in the 1890s showed how much force still stood behind the employers. At Carnegie's Homestead works in 1892, Henry Clay Frick brought hired Pinkerton guards up the river by barge. They fought an open gun battle with the strikers, as lesson 5.2 described. Two years later the Pullman Palace Car Company cut wages in its company town outside Chicago. It did not cut the rents it charged those same workers. Eugene V. Debs's American Railway Union answered with a boycott: its members would handle no train carrying a Pullman car. Rail traffic west of Chicago stopped.

The federal response set a lasting pattern. Attorney General Richard Olney obtained a court order commanding the union to stop, and he built the argument partly on the Sherman Antitrust Act. That law had been written against combinations like the trusts of lesson 4.1. It was now aimed at a combination of workers. President Cleveland sent troops on the ground that the boycott delayed the mail. Debs went to jail for defying the court order, and the Supreme Court upheld the sentence in 1895. He spent those months reading, and he came out a socialist. Week 11 follows where that led.

Alongside organized labor, a new kind of journalist appeared. These writers spent months or years documenting one industry's abuses in detail too specific to wave away. Material conditions mattered here as much as courage did. Fast presses, cheap paper, and photographs printed directly onto the page had brought a magazine's price down to ten cents. A magazine with several hundred thousand subscribers could afford to pay a reporter to work three years on one story. In January 1903, a single issue of McClure's Magazine carried Ida Tarbell on Standard Oil, Lincoln Steffens on city government, and Ray Stannard Baker on labor.

President Roosevelt found some of this writing excessive, and he gave the movement its lasting name in a 1906 speech. He compared these reporters to the man with the muck rake in Pilgrim's Progress. That man was so intent on raking filth that he never looked up to see the crown held out above him. The label was half insult and half compliment, and it stuck. Whatever Roosevelt's reservations, this reporting did something the strikes could not. It made the conduct of private power a matter of public record, which is where the next lesson begins.

🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain how the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, the Knights of Labor, the American Federation of Labor, and the Pullman boycott each represented a different response by organized labor to industrial power
2

3
