Ida Tarbell and Investigative Journalism

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Ida TarbellEYE-duh TAR-belA journalist whose detailed, documented investigation of Standard Oil helped build the public case that led to the company's 1911 breakup. was born in 1857 in the Pennsylvania oil country, and the oil business shaped her childhood directly. Her father Franklin built wooden storage tanks, then went into producing and refining oil himself at Titusville. In 1872 a scheme called the South Improvement Company promised a small group of refiners secret freight rebates. It also promised them drawbacks, paid out of their rivals' own shipping bills. Lesson 4.3 described how that arrangement worked and who designed it. The independent producers of the oil regions revolted, and the scheme collapsed within months. The pattern behind it did not collapse. Small refiners like her father were squeezed out over the years that followed, and Tarbell grew up watching it happen.

None of that made her later work an act of revenge, and she took care to say so. She trained herself as a historian instead. She graduated from Allegheny College in 1880, the only woman in her entering class. She taught for a time, then wrote and edited for a magazine called The Chautauquan. In 1891 she moved to Paris to research a biography of a figure from the French Revolution, paying her way by selling articles to American editors. One of those editors was Samuel McClure, who hired her. Her serial lives of Napoleon and then of Lincoln made her one of the best-known magazine writers in the country before she wrote a word about oil.

She spent about three years on it. She worked from state court records and from testimony taken by congressional and state investigating committees. The company's own papers came into her hands too, some of them handed over by a former employee. For roughly two years she interviewed Henry H. Rogers, a sitting Standard Oil executive. That relationship ended when she published what her records supported rather than what her source preferred. The decision cost her the access. It is exactly the sort of decision that determines whether a piece of reporting is worth anything at all.

This was the real innovation muckraking brought to American public life. Outrage was old. What was new was documentation specific enough that a company as powerful as Standard Oil could not simply deny it. Tarbell named the rebate and drawback arrangements. She named dates, shipping rates, towns, and the documents themselves. A reader who doubted her could go look. An accusation invites a denial and the argument ends there. A citation invites a check, and the check is what settles the matter.

Some of what she turned up was not about prices at all. She showed that Standard Oil had obtained regular reports on its competitors' shipments, supplied by clerks in railroad freight offices. An independent refiner could not send a barrel anywhere without the information reaching a rival. Buyers who dealt with independents found themselves visited by Standard salesmen soon afterward. That kind of finding did more damage than any complaint about rates could have done. It described not a hard bargainer but a company treating other people's business as its own to inspect.

She was not always right, and honesty requires saying so. Her account of the Backus buyout, one of her most damaging chapters, rests on evidence historians still argue over. She also held a position that surprises most students who meet her. She opposed votes for women, and published a book in 1912 arguing that a woman's proper work lay in the home. A person can be scrupulous about one question and mistaken about another. Treating her as a flawless hero would teach the wrong habit of reading, about her and about everyone else.

Tarbell also resisted the muckrakerMUK-ray-kerA journalist who spent extended time investigating and exposing corporate or political abuses. The term comes from a 1906 Theodore Roosevelt speech. label Roosevelt handed her, and her reason was consistent with her method. She insisted her aim was accuracy rather than vengeance. Whatever the label, she went on working the same slow documentary way.

Her reporting did not by itself break Standard Oil. The federal suit filed in 1906 drew on findings her installments had already made public. In 1911 the Supreme Court ordered the company divided into more than thirty parts. But consider what changed. The Sherman Antitrust Act had been on the books since 1890, and in 1895 the Court read it down almost to nothing, as lesson 4.1 described. The statute was the same in 1911. What had changed was what the public and the government understood about how the company actually operated. That understanding was built substantially out of nineteen magazine installments.

That opens a question worth sitting with. Suppose McClure's had never funded three years of one reporter's work. Ohio's courts had already ordered the trust dissolved in 1892, and pressure from the states was mounting on its own. Perhaps the breakup arrives anyway, a few years later. Perhaps the company reorganizes again, as it had done before, and the moment passes. The honest answer is that we cannot know, and that is the point. Outcomes like the 1911 decision are not delivered by history on a schedule. They are assembled out of particular acts by particular people, any of which might not have happened.

Judge her by the standard this course has been using. A political community needs some way to see itself clearly, because a public that cannot find out what is being done to it cannot deliberate about it. In these years the government had no bureau capable of that work, and Congress's investigating committees met only when they chose to. Tarbell held no office, commanded nothing, and could compel no one to answer her. She supplied a capacity the constitutional machinery lacked, using patience and a filing system. That is a real civic achievement.

It also had a real limit, and the limit matters as much as the achievement. Publicity by itself never lowered a price or shortened a shift. It took the Justice Department, the courts, and nine more years. Documentation makes action possible. It does not substitute for action.
