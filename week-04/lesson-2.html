📖 LESSON 4.3
Rockefeller and the Standard Oil Trust
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

John D. Rockefeller entered the oil refining business in Cleveland in 1863, just as the industry was chaotic, overbuilt, and prone to boom-and-bust price wars. His genius, and it was a genuine genius for solving an economic problem, lay in relentless attention to efficiency: he squeezed cost out of every barrel, every barrel stave, every rail car, and used the savings to underprice competitors until they sold to him rather than fail outright. By 1870 his Standard Oil Company controlled roughly a tenth of American refining; by the time he formalized the Standard Oil Trust in 1882, that share had grown to nearly ninety percent.

Rockefeller's methods were not purely a matter of superior efficiency, however, and this is what makes him a harder figure to judge in Classical Republican terms than Vanderbilt. In 1872 Rockefeller and a group of allied refiners formed the South Improvement Company, a secret scheme with major railroads to receive rebates on Standard's own shipments and, more damaging to competitors, drawbacks, secret kickbacks on the shipments of rival refiners who had no idea they were subsidizing the company driving them out of business. When the scheme became public, the resulting outrage forced its collapse within months, but Rockefeller had learned that raw efficiency, combined with a willingness to use secret leverage over shared infrastructure like railroads, could crush competitors that efficiency alone might have taken years to beat.

By the 1880s, Standard Oil's dominance was so complete that the company could dictate terms to the very railroads that had once given it secret rebates. Rockefeller himself gave away roughly half his fortune in his lifetime, funding medical research, education, and public health efforts whose consequences, most historians agree, saved and improved millions of lives. Whether that later philanthropy redeems the methods of the 1870s, or whether the two should be judged entirely separately, is exactly the kind of question a Classical Republican evaluation of virtue and vice as historical explanation should not shy away from asking directly.
