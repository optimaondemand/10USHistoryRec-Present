📖 LESSON 4.3
Rockefeller and the Standard Oil Trust
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

John D. Rockefeller entered the oil refining business in Cleveland in 1863, when the industry was chaotic, overbuilt, and prone to violent price swings. Petroleum had been drilled in western Pennsylvania for only four years. Anyone with a few thousand dollars could put up a still and call himself a refiner, and hundreds of men did. Prices collapsed whenever a new field came in and spiked whenever one ran dry. Rockefeller's location gave him an advantage he understood better than his rivals did. Cleveland sat on Lake Erie and had several competing rail connections. He could ship his kerosene by water or by any of several railroads. That choice let him play the carriers against one another for lower rates. A refiner in the Pennsylvania oil fields, served by a single railroad, had no such leverage.

His genius, and it was a genuine genius for solving an economic problem, lay in relentless attention to cost. He squeezed waste out of every barrel and every rail car. Rather than buy barrels, Standard Oil bought oak timberland and built its own cooperage. Rather than throw away the residue left after refining kerosene, the company sold it as lubricants, paraffin, and paving material. Rockefeller liked to tell a story about the machine that soldered lids onto kerosene cans. It used forty drops of solder. He asked whether thirty-nine would hold. Thirty-eight leaked. Thirty-nine did not, and across millions of cans the single saved drop mattered. This was not glamorous work, and it was not theft. It was the ordinary discipline of a man who read his own cost sheets every day. He expected everyone under him to do the same. By 1870 his Standard Oil Company controlled roughly a tenth of American refining.

Rockefeller's methods were not purely a matter of superior efficiency, however. That is what makes him a harder man to judge than Vanderbilt. In 1872 he and a group of allied refiners formed the South Improvement Company. It was a secret arrangement with the major railroads, and it had two parts. Standard would receive rebates, meaning discounts, on its own shipments. Far more damaging, Standard would also receive drawbacks: secret payments taken out of what rival refiners paid to ship their own oil. A competitor shipping kerosene east was unknowingly funding the company that was driving him out of business. When the terms leaked, oil producers in Pennsylvania rose in open revolt. They refused to sell crude to any member of the scheme. It collapsed within a few months. Rockefeller had lost the arrangement, but he had learned the lesson. Raw efficiency combined with secret leverage over shared infrastructure could crush competitors that efficiency alone might have taken years to beat.

He acted on it immediately. During the same few months of panic in early 1872, he went to the other refiners of Cleveland. He offered to buy them out, in cash or in Standard stock. The alternative was a price war he was fully prepared to wage. Twenty-two of the city's twenty-six refiners sold. Those who took Standard stock rather than cash generally grew rich. Those who took cash remembered the episode very differently. Among them was the family of a young girl in Titusville named Ida Tarbell. As an adult, Tarbell spent two years investigating the company for a magazine series that helped bring it down. This course returns to her in Week 7. By 1882, when Samuel Dodd drew up the Standard Oil Trust agreement, Rockefeller's share of American refining had grown from a tenth to roughly nine tenths.

The law caught up with him slowly, and never quite in the way his critics wanted. In 1892 the Ohio Supreme Court ordered the trust dissolved, so Rockefeller reorganized under New Jersey's friendly holding-company law and carried on. In 1911 the Supreme Court of the United States finally broke Standard Oil into thirty-four separate companies. The outcome carried an irony that says a great deal about the era. Rockefeller owned a quarter of the stock in every one of the successor companies. Their combined value rose sharply after the breakup. The decision made him, by some measures, the richest man the country had ever produced. None of this was fated. Suppose the Supreme Court had read the Sherman Act in 1895 the way it read the same words in 1911. The breakup would have come sixteen years sooner. Standard Oil might never have reached the size that made it a national grievance.

Judging Rockefeller in Classical Republican terms is genuinely difficult, and the difficulty is the point. In this course, a virtue is a stable trait of character that helps a person succeed at activities serving the common good. A vice is a stable trait that leads to failure at the same activities. Rockefeller possessed real virtues by that standard. He was temperate, methodical, patient, and almost entirely free of the appetite for display that ruined other men of his generation. Those traits solved a real problem. Kerosene that had cost around fifty-eight cents a gallon in 1865 sold for a small fraction of that by the 1890s. Ordinary families could light their homes after dark for the first time. He also possessed a settled willingness to treat shared infrastructure as a private weapon. He kept those arrangements hidden, because he knew perfectly well how they would look in daylight. That is a stable trait too. It worked directly against the common good of a people trying to govern their own economy by public law.

He gave away roughly half his fortune during his lifetime, and the giving was as systematic as the earning had been. He founded the University of Chicago and established the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research. He funded the General Education Board, which built schools across the rural South. He also paid for a campaign that came close to eliminating hookworm from the region. Most historians agree that this work saved and improved millions of lives. Does that later philanthropy redeem the methods of the 1870s? Or should the two records be weighed entirely separately? A serious evaluation of virtue and vice asks that question directly rather than avoiding it. Carnegie's Gospel of Wealth, which you will read later this week, argues one answer with great confidence. You are not obliged to accept it.

🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain how Rockefeller's relentless attention to efficiency, and his willingness to use secret railroad rebates and drawbacks, together built Standard Oil's near-total dominance of refining
2
I will be able to trace the legal contest over Standard Oil from the South Improvement Company through the 1911 Supreme Court breakup, and identify where the outcome might plausibly have gone otherwise
3
I will be able to evaluate whether Rockefeller's later philanthropy redeems or should be judged separately from the methods that built his fortune, using Classical Republican reasoning about virtue and vice
