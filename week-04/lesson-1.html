Big Business Consolidation, Managers, and Wage Earners

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Before the Civil War, most American businesses were small enough for one owner to run in person. A man who owned a mill knew his workers by name. He set his own prices, and he could see the whole operation from one end of the floor to the other. Railroads broke that pattern first. A railroad needed thousands of miles of track, thousands of employees, and far more capital than any single family fortune could supply. By the 1870s and 1880s, that same scale had spread into oil, steel, meatpacking, and a dozen other industries. Machines grew bigger and far more expensive. A factory that once cost a few thousand dollars to build now cost millions. Once an owner had sunk that much money into machinery, he could not simply stop producing when prices fell. He had to keep the machines running to pay for them. Every competitor faced the same pressure at the same moment. The result was ruinous price competition. Everyone cut prices, everyone kept producing, and everyone lost money.

Owners wanted a way to stop that competition, and the obvious answer was to merge into a single company. The law stood in the way. Corporations in this era were chartered by individual states, not by the federal government, and several states flatly forbade one corporation from owning stock in a rival. Lawyers went looking for a way around the rule. In 1882 one of them found it. Samuel C. T. DoddSAM-yoo-el dee tee dahdThe Standard Oil attorney who invented the trust device in 1882., an attorney for Standard Oil, borrowed an old device from property law: the trusttrustA legal arrangement in which stockholders of competing companies hand shares to a small board of trustees, letting that board set prices and output across an entire industry.. Stockholders in competing firms handed their shares over to a small board of trustees. In exchange they received trust certificates, which paid them dividends just as their old shares had. The underlying companies stayed legally separate, so no state law was broken. But a handful of trustees now set prices and output for an entire industry. The word "trust" had long meant a careful arrangement for managing a widow's inheritance. It now meant something close to the opposite.

What happened next was not inevitable, and it is worth watching closely. In 1889 New Jersey rewrote its corporation law to let companies chartered there hold stock in companies chartered anywhere else. New Jersey did this to collect charter fees, not to reorganize American industry, but the effect was enormous. Firms across the country reincorporated in New Jersey, and the awkward trust device was no longer even necessary. Congress answered in 1890 with the Sherman Antitrust ActSHER-mun AN-ty-trust aktAn 1890 federal law outlawing business combinations "in restraint of trade," passed almost unanimously but weakly enforced for its first decade., which passed almost unanimously and outlawed every combination "in restraint of trade." Those words were sweeping, and the courts could have read them that way. In 1895 the Supreme Court chose not to. In United States v. E. C. Knight Companyyoo-NY-ted stayts VER-sus ee see nyt KUM-puh-neeAn 1895 Supreme Court case holding that manufacturing lay outside the Sherman Act's reach, leaving the law nearly toothless for years., the Court held that a sugar combination controlling most American refining was engaged in manufacturing rather than in commerce between the states. Manufacturing, the Court said, lay outside the reach of the new law. That single reading left the Sherman Act nearly toothless for the next decade. Serious trust-busting had to wait for a different Court and a different president, a story this course takes up in Week 13.

Consolidation came in two shapes, and the difference matters for the men this week and next take up. Horizontal integrationhor-ih-ZAHN-tul in-tuh-GRAY-shunA business strategy of buying competitors at the same stage of production, as Rockefeller did with oil refining. meant buying competitors at the same stage of production: one refinery buying other refineries until it controlled most of the refining in the country. Vertical integrationVER-tih-kul in-tuh-GRAY-shunA business strategy of owning every stage of production, as Carnegie did with steel. meant owning every stage of the process instead, from the raw ore still in the ground to the finished rail leaving the mill. John D. Rockefeller built Standard Oil mainly the first way. Andrew Carnegie built Carnegie Steel mainly the second. Both strategies produced enormous firms, but they solved different problems. Horizontal integration let a company control the price. Vertical integration let a company control its own supply, so that no rival could squeeze it by cutting off something it needed.

Running an operation this large required a new kind of employee. The salaried professional manager owned no stock in the company he ran. He was hired to plan production, coordinate departments, and answer to owners he might never meet in person. Railroads had invented this role, and industry now borrowed it wholesale. A manager's authority came from his place on an organizational chart rather than from anything he owned. He kept cost sheets, measured one department's performance against another's, and reported upward in writing. This class had barely existed a generation earlier. It grew quickly, and it offered something genuinely new: a career. A young man could enter a large firm as a clerk and rise, step by step, to run a division. His security came from the company itself rather than from tools he owned or a trade he had learned.

Below the managers grew a far larger class of industrial wage earners. These workers neither owned tools nor sold a finished product. They sold their labor by the hour to a company that owned the factory, the machinery, and everything the machinery produced. An artisan who once might have run a small shop was now more likely to tend a single machine on a floor he did not own. The change was not only about income. A skilled cabinetmaker carried his trade with him and could take it to another town. A machine tender's skill was tied to that machine, in that mill, and it did not travel. Steel mills ran two twelve-hour shifts, six or seven days a week, and when the shifts changed, the men on the long turn worked around the clock. Pay was steady while the economy was strong. When it was not, the exposure was total. The depression that began in 1893 threw so many industrial workers out of their jobs that some estimates put unemployment near one in five. There was no unemployment insurance and no severance pay. A wage earner had his savings, his family, and whatever his neighbors could spare.

Both classes grew out of the same consolidation, and that consolidation deserves careful judgment rather than simple approval or condemnation. Measured against the economic problem of producing enough for a whole population, the trusts performed. They cut waste, standardized products, drove down the price of kerosene and steel, and put goods within reach of ordinary families that had been luxuries a generation earlier. Measured against the political problem of a community governing itself, they performed badly. A trust could be bigger than any one town, any one family fortune, and in the largest cases any one state's power to regulate it. A people unable to enforce their own laws against the most powerful actors inside their borders have a real political problem, whatever their factories happen to produce. Americans in the 1880s and 1890s felt both of these things at once, and the argument between them runs through the rest of this quarter. The next lesson takes up the man who built the largest trust of them all.
