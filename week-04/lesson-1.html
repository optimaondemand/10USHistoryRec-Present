📖 LESSON 4.1
Big Business Consolidation, Managers, and Wage Earners
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Before the Civil War, most American businesses were small enough for one owner to run personally. The scale railroads had already forced on themselves, thousands of miles of track, thousands of employees, capital needs no single family fortune could supply, spread by the 1870s and 1880s into oil, steel, meatpacking, and a dozen other industries. Owners who wanted to escape ruinous price competition found a legal device to stop it: several state laws forbade one corporation from simply owning stock in a rival, so lawyers instead built the "trust," an arrangement in which stockholders of competing firms handed their shares to a small board of trustees in exchange for trust certificates, letting a handful of men set prices and output for an entire industry without technically merging the underlying companies.

Running an operation this large required a new kind of employee: the salaried professional manager, someone who owned no stock in the company but was hired specifically to plan production, coordinate departments, and answer to distant owners rather than work alongside them. This managerial class had barely existed a generation earlier. Below it grew a much larger class of industrial wage earners, workers who increasingly neither owned tools nor sold a finished product themselves, but sold their labor by the hour to a company that owned the factory, the machinery, and the output. An artisan who once might have run his own small shop was now more likely to operate one machine on a factory floor he did not own and could not easily leave for another trade.

Both new classes grew directly out of the same consolidation. A managerial career offered social mobility that had not existed before, a path from clerk to executive inside a single large firm. A wage-earning job offered something more precarious: steady pay when the economy was strong, and total exposure to layoffs when it was not, with none of an artisan's old independence to fall back on. The trust made both possible by making the individual firm, for the first time, bigger than any one family, any one town, and in the largest cases, any one state's power to regulate it.
