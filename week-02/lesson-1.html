📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The Homestead Act of 1862HOHM-sted akt uv AY-teen SIKS-tee-tooA federal law offering 160 acres of public land free to any citizen who lived on and improved it for five years. offered any citizen, or any immigrant planning to become one, 160 acres of public land free of charge. The only requirement was to live on it and improve it for five years. On paper, it was an invitation to a nation of small farmers. In practice, it ran into a fact Congress had not solved by any law. The Great Plains already belonged, in every sense that mattered on the ground, to the horse-mounted nations who lived there. The Sioux, Cheyenne, Comanche, and their neighbors had spent more than a century mastering a mobile way of life built around the buffalo. That way of life let them strike, trade, and vanish across hundreds of miles of open grassland. No Homestead Act could be made real until that power was broken. Breaking it took the United States Army the better part of three decades. It also took several humiliating defeats and a deliberate campaign against the tribes' economic base: the buffalo herds, hunted toward extinction by the 1880s. Between 1862 and 1900, the government issued more than 1.6 million homestead claims. Barely half of all claimants ever proved up their land and kept it. Drought, isolation, and the sheer difficulty of prairie farming defeated as many families as they rewarded.

One treaty shows how quickly that power could be broken by a single event. It set aside the Black Hills of the Dakota Territory as part of a Great Sioux Reservation. The treaty promised this land would belong to the Lakota "as long as the grass shall grow." That promise lasted six years. But it changed little in the long run. Reinforced army columns broke Lakota resistance within a year, and Congress simply took the Black Hills by law in 1877.

Military defeat was not the end of the story for Plains nations. It was the beginning of a new federal policy meant to remake their way of life entirely. Reservations shrank further under the Dawes Act of 1887dawz akt uv AY-teen AY-tee-SEV-enA federal law breaking up communal tribal land into individual 160-acre plots and selling the remainder to non-Native buyers.. That law broke up communal tribal land into individual plots of 160 acres, the same size the Homestead Act offered white settlers, and sold whatever land was left over to non-Native buyers. The federal government also opened boarding schools far from tribal homelands. One example is the Carlisle Indian Industrial SchoolKAR-lyle IN-dee-un in-DUS-tree-ul skoolA boarding school founded in 1879 in Pennsylvania that separated Native children from their families to teach English and American customs in place of tribal languages. in Pennsylvania, founded in 1879. These schools separated Native children from their families to teach English, Christianity, and farming in place of their own languages and customs. Supporters of these policies argued they offered Native peoples a path to citizenship and property. In practice, the policies stripped tribes of most of their remaining land. They also did lasting damage to Native languages and family life across several generations.

The Indian Wars did not end with the Black Hills in 1877. For practical purposes, they ended in the winter of 1890 near Wounded KneeWOON-did neeThe site in South Dakota where, in December 1890, U.S. soldiers killed more than 150 Lakota men, women, and children, effectively ending the Indian Wars. Creek in South Dakota. By then, most Lakota lived on reservations far smaller than the Fort Laramie treaty had promised. Many had taken up the Ghost Dancegohst danssA religious movement among Plains nations that promised faithful dancing would restore the buffalo and drive out white settlers without violence., a religious movement that promised faithful dancing would restore the buffalo and drive out white settlers without any violence. Nervous federal agents saw the Ghost Dance as a warning sign of another uprising. They moved to arrest its leaders, including Sitting Bull, who was killed during his arrest in December 1890. Two weeks later, soldiers of the Seventh Cavalry, Custer's old regiment, surrounded a band of Lakota at Wounded Knee to disarm them. A shot rang out from an unclear source. The soldiers opened fire with rifles and rapid-fire guns, killing more than 150 Lakota men, women, and children. Wounded Knee closed, in the bluntest way possible, the military struggle this lesson has traced back to the Homestead Act's first promise of free land.

Technology did as much as policy to decide this larger contest. The repeating rifle gave the Army an advantage no earlier generation of soldiers had possessed. So did the railroads that could move troops and settlers alike. Joseph Glidden's barbed wirebarbd wyrFencing wire with sharp barbs, patented by Joseph Glidden in 1874, that let farmers cheaply enclose land and ended the era of open, unfenced cattle range., patented in 1874, let a farmer with no timber for a fence enclose 160 acres cheaply. It ended the era of open, unfenced range almost as soon as it had properly begun. Railroads received their own land grants from Congress, often in a checkerboard pattern of alternating square-mile sections. The railroads then sold that land to the very settlers they needed as future customers. This tied the fate of Plains farming to the fate of the railroad companies from the very start. German, Scandinavian, and Bohemian immigrants poured onto the plains behind the railroads, drawn by cheap land and free passage. Whole counties in Nebraska and the Dakotas took on the language and church customs of Central Europe within a single generation.

Not every settler who came to the Plains was foreign-born or free of the South's politics. In 1879, thousands of Black Southerners migrated to Kansas in a movement later called the Exodus. Many were discouraged by the collapse of Reconstruction traced in Week 1. The movement was led in part by a former Tennessee carpenter named Benjamin "Pap" Singleton. These ExodustersEK-suh-DUS-terzBlack Southerners who migrated to Kansas beginning in 1879, led in part by Benjamin "Pap" Singleton, seeking free land and escape from Redemption-era restrictions. saw the Homestead Act's promise of free land as a real escape from Redemption's new restrictions. Kansas land was often the driest and least developed land still available by the time they arrived. Their migration was small next to the millions of European immigrants who came west by rail. But it shows that the West's promise of free land was never only a story about foreign immigration. It was also a chapter in the still-unfinished argument over what freedom would mean for Black Americans after Reconstruction.

Between the buffalo's fall and the farmer's fence lay one more economy, brief but unforgettable: the open-range cattle drive. Texas longhorns, descended from Spanish stock and multiplying wild for decades, were worth only a few dollars a head in Texas. The same animal was worth forty dollars a head at a railhead in Kansas. Cowboys, many of them Black or Mexican veterans of the cattle trade, drove herds of a thousand or more up trails like the ChisholmCHIZ-um traylOne of the major cattle-drive routes used to move Texas longhorns north to railroad towns in Kansas. to meet the railroad. It was a decade-long boom. Barbed wire and a catastrophic winter in 1886-87 brought it to a fairly sudden end. None of it, from the Army's slow campaigns to the cowboy's brief golden age to the Exodusters' hard choice, followed any fixed schedule. It was a contest for the same ground among several different ways of life. Weather, weapons, gold, and railroads decided its outcome as much as any law passed in Washington.
