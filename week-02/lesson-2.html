The West & Agrarian America
📖 LESSON 2.3
The Grange Movement and Agrarian Populism
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Success in wheat and corn brought its own trap. As Plains farmers put more acres under the plow, grain prices fell year after year through the 1870s and 1880s, both at home and around the world. The more successfully American farmers produced, the less each bushel was worth. Wheat that sold for roughly a dollar a bushel just after the Civil War brought as little as forty or fifty cents a bushel by the mid-1880s. The actual cost of farming, seed, machinery, and transportation, stayed the same or even rose. At the same time, farmers depended entirely on railroads to move their grain to market. Where only one railroad served a region, it could charge freight rates a farmer had no power to negotiate. Railroads made the problem worse through a pricing trick called long-haul, short-haul discrimination. A shipper on a busy route with several competing lines might pay less to move goods a thousand miles than a farmer on an isolated branch line paid to move the same goods only a hundred miles. The isolated farmer simply had no other railroad to threaten his business away from. A farmer also depended on credit to buy seed and equipment before harvest. A currency still tied to a limited gold supply made this worse: debts taken out in inflated wartime dollars often had to be repaid later in dollars worth far more, a hidden tax on every debtor farmer that no law had ever intended.

Farmers noticed the currency problem well before the Grange organized around railroads specifically. During the Civil War, the government had issued paper "greenback" dollars not backed by gold to help pay for the fighting. After the war ended, creditors and much of the business world pushed to return fully to the gold standard. That change would shrink the money supply and make every dollar of debt harder to repay. Farmers wanted more currency in circulation, not less. In 1874 they organized their own Greenback Party. Its 1876 presidential candidate, the elderly industrialist Peter Cooper, won only a small share of the vote. The Greenback Party itself did not last. But its central argument, that a shrinking money supply favored creditors over debtors, would resurface almost unchanged in the Populists' later demand for free silver.

The Grange, formally the Patrons of Husbandry, was founded in 1867 by Oliver Hudson Kelley. Kelley was a clerk in the federal Department of Agriculture who had toured the defeated South. He came away convinced that farm families everywhere, not only in the South, suffered from isolation and a lack of shared information. The Grange began as a social and educational organization for isolated farm families. Its local chapters quickly became a vehicle for collective economic action. Granges organized cooperative grain elevators and purchasing societies to bypass middlemen. In several Midwestern states, they successfully lobbied state legislatures to pass "Granger Laws" capping the rates railroads and grain warehouses could charge. Grange halls also became rare social centers of prairie life, hosting dances, lectures, and mutual insurance societies in communities otherwise separated by miles of open land. Unlike most organizations of its time, the Grange admitted women as full voting members from its founding. That decision gave farm wives a direct political voice years before most American women could vote at all. Railroads challenged the Granger Laws in court. In Munn v. Illinois (1877), the Supreme Court upheld a state's power to regulate a business "affected with a public interest," a rule that shaped American regulatory law well into the twentieth century.

That state-level victory did not last. In the Wabash case of 1886, the Supreme Court reversed course. It ruled that states could not regulate railroad rates on shipments crossing state lines, since only Congress held that power under the Constitution. Farm-state congressmen, many of them elected with Alliance and Grange support, pushed hard for a federal response. Without that pressure, Congress might have left the matter to the states indefinitely. The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887, passed within a year of the ruling, created the first federal agency to regulate a private industry: the Interstate Commerce Commission. It required that railroad rates be "reasonable and just." The Wabash ruling shows how fragile the Grange's earlier success really was. A state-by-state strategy that had worked through the 1870s stopped working the moment the Supreme Court closed that door. Only a new federal law reopened it.

The Grange's own political momentum faded by the late 1870s. But the grievances it had organized did not disappear. They migrated into the Farmers' Alliances of the 1880s, which built the same kind of cooperative institutions on a larger, more openly political scale. By 1890, Alliance-backed candidates had won control of several state legislatures and a share of seats in Congress. In 1892, Alliance leaders and their allies founded a full national Populist Party. Its Omaha Platform demanded the free coinage of silver alongside gold to expand the money supply. It also called for a graduated income tax, direct election of senators by voters rather than state legislatures, an eight-hour workday, and government ownership of the railroads and telegraph lines. That year, Union Army veteran James B. Weaver ran as the Populist Party's first presidential candidate. He carried five Western states and won 22 electoral votes, a genuine breakthrough for a party barely a year old, even though it fell far short of the White House. Populist speakers carried this platform across the country with real passion. The Kansas orator Mary Elizabeth Lease is remembered, though the exact wording is disputed, for urging farmers to "raise less corn and more hell."

None of these later developments were guaranteed by the Grange's founding in 1867. A Supreme Court that upheld Munn indefinitely, a Congress less willing to pass the Interstate Commerce Act, or farmers less willing to organize across state lines could each have kept agrarian protest local and easy to ignore. What the Grange did prove, a full generation before the Populists, was simple: isolated, individually powerless farmers could act as an organized political and economic force once their common interest became clear enough. The Populist Party never won the presidency on its own platform. But by 1896, its central demand, free silver, would capture the entire Democratic Party and its nominee, a young Nebraska congressman named William Jennings Bryan, whom this course returns to in Week 16. That 1896 campaign, and Bryan's famous speech at the Democratic convention, belong to a later lesson. But its roots reach back directly to the Grange halls and Alliance meetings this lesson has traced.
