Hetch Hetchy: One Valley, Two Verdicts

Read both real arguments below. Before you find out what Congress actually decided in 1913, commit to your own verdict.

John Muir's case

Hetch Hetchy Valley is nearly as magnificent as Yosemite Valley itself. Damming it for San Francisco's water supply would destroy an irreplaceable wild place forever, something no later generation could ever get back.

Gifford Pinchot's case

A growing city's need for a reliable water supply is a legitimate "wise use" of a natural resource. San Francisco's devastating 1906 earthquake and fire left the city desperate for water it could actually count on, and no other nearby site could supply it as cheaply.

What Congress actually decided Congress authorized the dam in 1913, and it was built soon after. Muir died the following year; some of his own friends believed losing Hetch Hetchy hastened his decline. Judged by this course's own standard for evaluating a genuine first-order economic problem, how a growing society should use a scarce resource, neither Muir nor Pinchot was simply wrong. The argument did not end with the dam. It is the direct ancestor of wilderness-versus-managed-use fights that still shape American public land policy today.
