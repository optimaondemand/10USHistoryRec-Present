📖 LESSON 2.3
The Grange Movement and Agrarian Populism
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Success in wheat and corn brought its own trap. As Plains farmers put more acres under the plow, national and even world grain prices fell, year after year, through the 1870s and 1880s: the more successfully American farmers produced, the less each bushel was worth. At the same time, farmers depended entirely on railroads to move that grain to market, and where only one railroad served a region, it could charge freight rates a farmer had no power to negotiate. A farmer also depended on credit to buy seed and equipment before harvest, and a currency still tied to a limited gold supply meant that debts taken out in inflated dollars often had to be repaid in dollars that were worth more than when the debt was made.

The Grange (formally the Patrons of Husbandry, founded in 1867) began as a social and educational organization for isolated farm families, but its local chapters quickly became a vehicle for collective economic action. Granges organized cooperative grain elevators and purchasing societies to bypass middlemen, and in several Midwestern states they successfully lobbied state legislatures to pass "Granger Laws" capping the rates railroads and grain warehouses could charge. Railroads challenged these laws in court, and in Munn v. Illinois (1877) the Supreme Court upheld a state's power to regulate a business "affected with a public interest," a doctrine that would shape American regulatory law well into the twentieth century.

The Grange's own political momentum faded by the late 1870s, but the grievances it had organized did not disappear; they migrated into the Farmers' Alliances of the 1880s and, by 1892, into a full national Populist Party demanding the free coinage of silver, a graduated income tax, and direct election of senators. None of these later developments were guaranteed by the Grange's founding in 1867. What the Grange did prove, a full generation before the Populists, was that isolated, individually powerless farmers could act as an organized political and economic force when their common interest was clear enough.
