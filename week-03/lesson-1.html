📖 TODAY'S LESSON

A railroad is only as good as its rails. For the first decades of American railroading, rails were made of iron, which cracked and wore flat within two or three years of hard use. The Bessemer processBES-uh-mer PRAH-sessA method of blasting air through molten iron to burn off impurities, making steel cheap enough to mass-produce. blasted air through molten iron to burn off its impurities. It made steel cheap enough to roll into rails by the thousands of miles. Steel had once been a costly specialty metal used mainly for tools and swords. The process took its name from the English engineer Henry BessemerHEN-ree BES-uh-merThe English engineer who patented the Bessemer process in 1856., who patented it in 1856. But an American ironmaster named William KellyWIL-yum KEL-eeAn American ironmaster who independently discovered a similar steelmaking technique but lacked the money to develop it commercially. had discovered much the same technique several years earlier in Kentucky. Kelly lacked the money and the reputation to build his discovery into an industry. His own patent ran into financial trouble, so Bessemer's better-funded and better-known version became the one steelmakers across the world adopted and remembered. Which of the two men actually deserved more credit was never really the point. The process spread because it was cheap enough to use, not because history had already decided whose name belonged on it. A steel rail could last a decade instead of two years. The price of a ton of steel rail fell from well over a hundred dollars in the late 1860s to under twenty dollars by the 1890s. That single change in cost, more than any single law or entrepreneur, is a large part of why the two decades after the Civil War are remembered as the start of a Second Industrial RevolutionSEK-und in-DUS-tree-ul rev-oh-LOO-shunThe decades after the Civil War marked by rapid growth in steel, railroads, and heavy industry, driven by cheap steel.. American railroad mileage roughly quadrupled between 1865 and 1890, and steel production increased more than a hundredfold in the same years.

Steel's falling price did not stay contained to railroads. Cheap steel needed cheap coal to fuel the furnaces that made it, and together the two created entire new industries almost overnight. Coal mining expanded across Pennsylvania and West Virginia to feed blast furnaces. A new kind of industrial city grew up wherever coal, iron ore, and rail lines met. Pittsburgh sat near both coal and iron ore deposits and was served by several rail lines. It grew into the nation's steel capital during these same years, a story this course returns to in Week 5. Pittsburgh's location mattered too: the Allegheny and Monongahela Rivers meet there to form the Ohio, giving the city cheap water transport for heavy raw materials that no inland city without a river could match. None of this growth was planned by any single government policy. It followed the incentives created by cheap transportation and cheap fuel, the same way barbed wire and the repeating rifle, discussed in Week 2, reshaped the contest for the Great Plains without anyone deliberately choosing that outcome in advance.

The railroad that captured the nation's imagination was the transcontinental line, chartered by Congress in the Pacific Railroad Act of 1862puh-SIF-ik RAYL-rohd akt uv AY-teen SIKS-tee-tooThe law chartering the transcontinental railroad, with routes and funding set by Congress.. Congress had argued for years before the Civil War over which route a transcontinental railroad should take. A Southern route through Texas and a Northern or central route through Nebraska each promised huge profits and settlement to whichever region won. Southern senators and representatives had blocked a Northern route for a decade. Once those states seceded and their representatives left Congress, the central route passed almost without a fight. This is a plain example of how the Civil War itself, not any change of engineering opinion, decided where the tracks would run. The engineer who first proved a central route through the Sierra Nevada was possible was Theodore JudahTHEE-oh-dor JOO-duhThe civil engineer who proved a central route through the Sierra Nevada was possible and lobbied Congress for the transcontinental railroad charter., a young civil engineer who spent years surveying the mountains and lobbying Congress for a charter. Judah died of yellow fever in 1863, six years before the golden spikeGOHL-den spykThe ceremonial final spike driven at Promontory Summit, Utah, on May 10, 1869, marking the transcontinental railroad's completion. he had made possible was ever driven. That spike was driven on May 10, 1869, when the Union Pacific building west from Nebraska met the Central Pacific building east from California at Promontory Summit, Utah.

The Central Pacific's grade through the Sierras was cut largely by Chinese immigrant laborers. They worked with hand tools and explosives in brutal winter conditions, often for lower pay than white workers doing the same job. The Union Pacific's plains route relied heavily on Irish immigrant and Civil War veteran labor working under similarly dangerous conditions. Wages for this work ran to roughly one to three dollars for a twelve-hour day, six days a week. The exact number of workers who died building the line is still debated by historians today, especially for the Chinese laborers whose deaths often went unrecorded by their employers. Congress paid each company in government bonds. The rate was sixteen thousand dollars per mile of track across flat land, and up to forty-eight thousand dollars per mile through the mountains. Each company also received twenty square miles of land grants for every mile completed. This arrangement rewarded companies for choosing longer, more mountainous routes over shorter, more direct ones. It rewarded speed above almost everything else, including safety. As the Crédit Mobilier scandal from Week 1 already showed, it invited fraud on a matching scale. Railroad workers did not accept every cost of that speed quietly. In 1877, several lines cut wages for the second time in a year. Workers walked off the job in the Great Railroad Strikegrayt RAYL-rohd stryk uv AY-teen SEV-en-tee-SEV-enThe first nationwide labor uprising in American history, triggered by repeated railroad wage cuts., the first nationwide labor uprising in American history, a foretaste of the labor conflict Week 7 examines in full. The same country that depended on Chinese labor to build its most celebrated railroad would soon turn against Chinese immigrants by law. Within twenty years, Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882chy-NEEZ ex-KLOO-zhun akt uv AY-teen AY-tee-tooThe first federal law to bar immigration by name based on national origin, targeting Chinese immigrants.. It was the first federal law to bar immigration by name based on national origin, a contradiction this course will return to.

None of this made railroads simply good or simply corrupt. They cut a journey that had taken months by wagon down to about a week. They bound California's economy to the rest of the nation for the first time. They also created the first American businesses large enough to require professional, salaried managers rather than owner-operators. This shift to hired managers was a revolution nearly as important as the steel itself. Running trains safely across thousands of miles on a fixed schedule required layers of supervisors, clerks, and engineers that no earlier American business had ever needed. The methods railroads invented to manage that complexity would later spread to steel mills, oil refineries, and factories of every kind. Railroads also produced fortunes and failures on a scale the country had never seen. The difference between the men who built something real with those fortunes and the men who merely extracted them is the subject of the next lesson.
