📖 LESSON 3.1
Gilded Age: Rise of Heavy Industry
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

A railroad is only as good as its rails, and for the first decades of American railroading, rails were made of iron, which cracked and wore flat within two or three years of hard use. The Bessemer process, which blasted air through molten iron to burn off impurities, made steel, previously a costly specialty metal, cheap enough to roll into rails by the thousands of miles. A steel rail could last a decade instead of two years. That single change, more than any single law or entrepreneur, is why the two decades after the Civil War are remembered as the start of a Second Industrial Revolution: American railroad mileage roughly quadrupled between 1865 and 1890, and steel production increased more than a hundredfold in the same years.

The railroad that captured the nation's imagination was the transcontinental line, chartered by Congress in 1862 and finished on May 10, 1869, when the Union Pacific building west from Nebraska met the Central Pacific building east from California at Promontory Summit, Utah. The engineer who first proved the route through the Sierra Nevada was Theodore Judah, a young civil engineer who spent years surveying the mountains and lobbying Congress for a charter, only to die of yellow fever in 1863, six years before the golden spike he had made possible was ever driven. The Central Pacific's grade through the Sierras was cut largely by Chinese immigrant laborers working with hand tools and explosives in brutal winter conditions; the Union Pacific's plains route relied heavily on Irish immigrant and Civil War veteran labor. Both companies were paid in government land grants and bonds calculated per mile of track laid, an incentive structure that rewarded speed and, as the Crédit Mobilier scandal from Week 1 already showed, invited fraud on a matching scale.

None of this made railroads simply good or simply corrupt. They cut a journey that had taken months by wagon down to about a week, bound California's economy to the rest of the nation for the first time, and created the first American businesses large enough to require professional, salaried managers rather than owner-operators, a managerial revolution nearly as important as the steel itself. They also produced fortunes and failures on a scale the country had never seen, and the difference between the men who built something real with those fortunes and the men who merely extracted them is the subject of the next lesson.
