📖 TODAY'S LESSON

Cornelius Vanderbiltkor-NEEL-yus VAN-der-biltA shipping and railroad magnate nicknamed "the Commodore" who built the New York Central railroad through efficient consolidation. earned the nickname "the Commodore" building a fortune in steamships and ferries before he ever owned a mile of railroad track. He had made that first fortune decades earlier by underpricing rival ferry lines on the Hudson River. He kept cutting prices until they either failed or paid him to leave a route alone. It was a tactic of ruthless price competition, and he would later bring the same tactic to railroads unchanged. He was already in his early seventies, an age when most successful businessmen of his era had retired, when he turned to buying up railroads instead. Between 1867 and 1869 he bought and combined a tangle of competing short lines running along the Hudson River and across upstate New York. Together they became a single trunk line, the New York Centralnoo york SEN-trulThe dominant railroad line between New York City and Chicago, built by Vanderbilt through consolidation of smaller lines., run efficiently enough to become the dominant carrier between New York City and Chicago. Vanderbilt's method was blunt. He bought control, cut costs, ran the trains on schedule, and let genuinely better service undercut weaker competitors until they sold out or failed.

Not every railroad man of the era competed by running a better railroad. The term itself came from Drew's earlier career as a cattle drover. Drovers were known to feed cattle salt and let them drink heavily just before a sale so the animals would weigh more. Drew brought that same trick, inflating a value that was not really there, from the cattle yard to Wall Street. A friendly New York judge issued an arrest warrant against the three men for the scheme. Drew, Gould, and Fisk fled across the Hudson River to Jersey City, New Jersey, reportedly carrying suitcases stuffed with millions of dollars in cash. There they set up a rival headquarters, safely outside the New York court's reach. From New Jersey, they bribed the state legislature to legalize the stock they had already issued, while Vanderbilt's own allies worked to bribe New York's legislature in the opposite direction. The fight ended not in court but in a private settlement. Vanderbilt sold his Erie shares back to the company at a loss and walked away, choosing to protect the New York Central rather than keep fighting for a railroad he no longer trusted.

The comparison between Vanderbilt and men like Gould is instructive in Classical Republican terms. Both men were called robber barons in their own time, and both grew immensely rich, but they got there in very different ways. Vanderbilt's virtue, if the word applies, was a genuine skill at solving the underlying economic problem: moving people and freight efficiently and reliably. Gould's fortune, by contrast, came from taking value out of a company without adding any, through watered stock, bribed lawmakers, and manipulated share prices. In the language this course uses for judging economic behavior, Vanderbilt helped solve America's first-order economic problem of moving goods and people efficiently across a continent. Gould's fortune solved no such problem for anyone but himself. One man left behind a railroad that worked better than the one he found. The other left behind a wrecked one and a reputation, even among fellow financiers, as a man whose word could not be trusted. Their difference is not really about which man broke more laws in an era with famously weak corporate regulation. It is a difference in what each man's success actually depended on.

The scale these companies reached forced a further change that had nothing to do with any one man's virtue or vice. A business managing thousands of miles of track, tens of thousands of employees, and schedules across several states could no longer be run the way a family workshop was run. Railroads pioneered the modern corporate hierarchy of salaried managers and standardized accounting, and they even standardized time itself. Much of this managerial method was pioneered not at Vanderbilt's own railroad but at a rival, the Pennsylvania Railroad. Its president, J. He divided the company into geographic divisions, each run by its own general superintendent reporting up a clear chain of command. Before 1883, American towns each kept their own local solar time, set by whenever the sun stood directly overhead in that particular place. A traveler crossing a few states by train might need to reset his watch twenty times over a single trip. Two trains running on two different local clocks on the same track was a real cause of deadly collisions. On November 18, 1883, a day newspapers nicknamed "the day of two noons," American and Canadian railroads imposed four standard time zonesSTAN-derd tym zohnzThe four time zones American and Canadian railroads imposed across the continent in 1883 to replace inconsistent local solar time. across the continent by mutual agreement among themselves. No act of Congress forced the change. A workable national rail schedule was simply impossible without one. The federal government did not formally adopt those same time zones into law until 1918, thirty-five years after the railroads had already made the choice for the entire country.

Vanderbilt marked his consolidation with a physical monument as well. In 1871 he opened Grand Central Depot in Manhattan, at the time the largest train station in the country. It was built specifically to unite the separate lines he had spent four years buying and combining under one roof. Vanderbilt died in 1877 leaving a fortune of about 100 million dollars, at the time the largest ever built by a single American, and a railroad that kept running profitably for his heirs long after his death. Gould, despite his tactics, was not finished either. He went on to build a rival telegraph and railroad empire of his own in later years, proof that even a reputation for bad faith did not necessarily end a Gilded Age career the way it might end one today. The tension between Vanderbilt's method and Gould's would repeat itself across the whole Gilded Age, in the oil industry, in steel, and in banking. Again and again, an ambitious man had to choose between building something the country actually needed and simply capturing value that already existed. The next lesson takes up that same tension in the industry where it became most consequential: oil, and the rise of John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil trust.
