📖 LESSON 8.3
Protestantism Mobilized: Temperance and the American Missionary Association
📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The Woman's Christian Temperance Union, founded in 1874 and led by Frances Willard from 1879 until her death in 1898, began with a narrow target, alcohol, and a narrow membership, church women. Willard transformed both. Under her "Do Everything" policy, WCTU chapters took up prison reform, labor conditions, public health, and women's suffrage alongside temperance, treating alcohol abuse as one symptom of a broader set of social ills that organized Christian women were equipped to address. By the 1890s the WCTU was the largest women's organization in the country, and its network of local chapters gave many American women their first sustained experience in public organizing and political advocacy, well before most of them could vote.

A parallel mobilization reached back to Reconstruction itself. The American Missionary Association, founded before the Civil War by abolitionist Protestants, had redirected much of its energy after 1865 toward educating the freedpeople this course examined in Week 1, founding schools and colleges across the South, including Fisk University and Hampton Institute, at a time when Southern state governments provided freedpeople's education grudgingly or not at all. The same organization simultaneously supported missionary work among Native Americans and, following the pattern described in the previous lesson, expanded overseas.

What connects Willard's temperance crusade and the AMA's freedpeople's schools is not a single doctrine but a common Protestant conviction, carried by ordinary church members rather than imposed by any government, that organized religious energy had a duty to remake unjust social conditions rather than simply preach around them. That conviction would find its fullest theological expression in the Social Gospel movement this week's second primary source represents.
