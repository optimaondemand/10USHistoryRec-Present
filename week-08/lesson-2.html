Protestantism Mobilized: Temperance, Freedpeople's Schools, and the Closing of the New South

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The Woman's Christian Temperance UnionWOO-munz KRIS-chun TEM-puh-runss YOON-yunA reform organization, led by Frances Willard, that grew from opposing alcohol into the era's largest women's organization. began in the winter of 1873, when women in Hillsboro, Ohio, walked into saloons and prayed until the owners closed. The movement spread through hundreds of towns within months. Delegates organized the national union at Cleveland in 1874. Frances WillardFRAN-siss WIL-erdThe leader of the WCTU from 1879 to 1898, whose "Do Everything" policy expanded the group's causes far beyond temperance. led it from 1879 until her death in 1898, and she changed what it was.

The union began with one target, alcohol, and one membership, church women. Willard's "Do Everything" policy widened both. Chapters took up prison reform, factory conditions, public health, and votes for women alongside temperance. Her argument for the ballot was practical: a woman with a vote could better protect her household. By the 1890s the WCTU was the largest women's organization in the country. Its chapters gave American women their first sustained experience of committees, petitions, and public speech. Most of them still could not vote. Her own account is this week's first primary source.

A second mobilization reached back further. The American Missionary Associationuh-MAIR-ih-kun MISH-uh-nair-ee uh-soh-see-AY-shunAn abolitionist-founded organization that built hundreds of schools and colleges for freedpeople across the South after the Civil War. had been founded in 1846 by Protestants who had defended the captives of the Amistad, and its roots were abolitionist. After 1865 it turned much of its strength toward educating the freedpeople of Week 1. It founded hundreds of schools across the South, and colleges: Fisk in 1866, Hampton in 1868 under Samuel Chapman Armstrong, Talladega, Tougaloo, Atlanta University. Northern teachers went south to staff them, many of them young women. Some found their schoolhouses burned. They kept teaching. Southern state governments funded Black schools grudgingly or not at all.

Week 1's second lesson ended in 1877, with the last federal troops withdrawn and Redeemer governments taking control of every former Confederate state. It promised that the fuller story of what Redemption meant would come here. This is that story, and it is the world the graduates of those schools walked into.

Start with land, because everything else rests on it. No land had been distributed. Sherman's wartime field order setting aside coastal acreage for freed families was revoked within months, as lesson 1.1 described, and the land went back to its former owners. So most freedpeople farmed ground they did not own, under an arrangement called sharecroppingSHAIR-krah-pingA farming arrangement in which a landless farmer works land owned by another in exchange for a share of the crop.. The landowner supplied land, seed, tools, and a cabin. The family supplied the labor. They split the crop at harvest.

The arrangement was closed tight by a second one, the crop lienkrahp leenA system in which a merchant advanced credit against a farmer's future crop at high interest, often trapping farmers in permanent debt.. A country merchant advanced food, cloth, and supplies through the year against the coming crop. He charged credit prices no cash buyer paid, plus interest. He kept the books, and settlement came once a year, when the cotton was sold and he told the family what it had earned. Cotton prices fell through the 1870s and again in the 1890s, the same collapse that drove the white farmers of Week 2 into the Grange and the Alliance. A family could work a full year, raise a good crop, and finish deeper in debt than it started. By 1900 about three in four Black farmers in the South owned no land.

Debt that carries forward becomes something worse than debt. Southern legislatures made it a crime to leave an employer while owing him money, and a crime for another employer to hire away a man under contract. A sheriff could return a laborer to the field he had left. Historians call this debt peonagedet PEE-uh-nijA system that used the force of law to hold workers to an employer as long as they owed him money.. It held people in place with the force of law rather than a bill of sale. The Supreme Court struck down Alabama's peonage statute in 1911. The practice outlived the ruling by decades.

Convict leasing extended the vagrancy laws lesson 1.3 described. A Black man who could not show proof of employment could be arrested for vagrancy and convicted. The state then leased him out to a coal mine, a turpentine camp, or a railroad grade, and collected the fee. The lessee had no stake in keeping a man alive past his sentence, and it showed. Annual death rates in some camps ran above one prisoner in ten.

Then the vote went. Mississippi's constitutional convention of 1890 wrote in a poll taxpohl taks / LIT-er-uh-see testFees and reading requirements used in the South to disqualify Black voters without naming race directly. and a literacy test. It added an "understanding clause" that let a registrar judge whether an applicant had explained a constitutional passage well enough. South Carolina followed in 1895. Louisiana added a grandfather clauseGRAND-fah-ther klawzA voting-law exemption for men whose fathers or grandfathers had been eligible to vote before Reconstruction, shielding white voters from new restrictions while excluding Black voters. in 1898, exempting men whose fathers had been qualified to vote before 1867. Read these laws and you will not find the word race. Read the debates and you will find nothing else. In Louisiana, registered Black voters fell from more than 130,000 in 1896 to about 1,300 by 1904.

The courts supplied the frame. Homer PlessyHOH-mer PLESS-eeThe man who deliberately boarded a segregated railroad car in 1892 to test Louisiana's segregation law in court. boarded a Louisiana railroad car in 1892 deliberately, to test a segregation statute in court. In Plessy v. FergusonPLESS-ee VER-sus FER-guh-sunAn 1896 Supreme Court case upholding racial segregation under the doctrine of "separate but equal." in 1896, the Supreme Court upheld the law, holding that separate accommodations could satisfy the Fourteenth Amendment as long as they were equal. Justice John Marshall Harlanjahn MAR-shul HAR-lunThe Supreme Court justice who alone dissented in Plessy v. Ferguson, arguing the Constitution is "color blind." dissented alone. He wrote that the Constitution is color blind, and that everyone understood perfectly well what the statute was for. A second reading of the same amendment sat on the page in a colleague's opinion. Eight justices declined it. Later historians named these decades the Nadirthuh NAY-derThe historians' term for the lowest point of Black Americans' political and civil status, roughly the 1890s to 1900s., meaning the lowest point.

Mobs killed several thousand Black Americans in these years, most often in the South. The killings were frequently announced in advance and almost never prosecuted. In March 1892 a Memphis mob murdered Thomas Moss and two partners, whose grocery had drawn customers away from a white competitor. Moss was Ida B. WellsEYE-duh bee welzA journalist and investigator who documented lynching cases in detail after a friend's murder, showing many accusations behind lynchings were false.'s friend. His death turned a teacher and editor into the era's most formidable investigator. Wells did to lynching what Tarbell did to Standard Oil in the previous lesson. She collected cases, names, dates, and the confessions printed in white newspapers themselves. In a large share of the killings, she showed, the alleged crime had never occurred at all. Her Memphis press was wrecked and she was warned never to return. She published Southern Horrors from the North in 1892 and carried her evidence to Britain.

Here the era's Protestant reform network has to be judged rather than admired. The WCTU had chapters in nearly every state and a president who told them to do everything. It did not take up lynching. Willard, then building membership among white Southern women, gave an interview in 1890 that repeated white Southerners' claims about Black men and drink. Wells quoted those words back at her publicly, in Britain, in 1894. Willard's answer was mostly defensive, and the WCTU never became an anti-lynching organization. Recall what virtue means in this course: a settled trait that helps a person succeed at work for the common good. Willard's organizing gifts were extraordinary and her accomplishments were real. Faced with the plainest injustice in her own country in her own lifetime, she protected the membership rolls.

None of this was required by the surrender at Appomattox or by the bargain of 1877. In 1890 a federal elections bill that would have put federal supervisors over congressional elections passed the House. It died in the Senate early the next year by a handful of votes. Had it passed, the disfranchising constitutions of the following decade would have had to operate against active federal supervision. The machinery of enforcement had not vanished. It was put down.

What connects Willard's crusade to the freedpeople's schools is not one doctrine. It is a shared Protestant conviction, carried by ordinary church members rather than imposed by any government. Organized religious energy, they held, owed the world a duty to remake unjust conditions rather than preach around them. These decades show what that conviction built and where it stopped short. Its fullest statement is the Social Gospel of this week's second primary source. Read it with this lesson in mind.
