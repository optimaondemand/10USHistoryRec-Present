Revivalism, Urban Poverty, and Foreign Missions

📖 TODAY'S LESSON

The industrial city produced its own religious response, and that response was not obvious in advance. A church looking at the crowded wards described in lesson 5.1 could reasonably have written them off. The people there moved often, worked long hours, spoke many languages, and belonged to no congregation. Dwight L. Moodydwyt el MOO-deeAn urban revivalist who treated the industrial city as a mission field, using organized, modern methods to reach working people. made the opposite choice, and the choice shaped a generation.

Moody was born in Massachusetts in 1837 and went to work selling shoes, first in Boston and then in Chicago. He was a salesman before he was anything else, and it showed in his methods. He gathered children from a poor Chicago district into a Sunday school, then rose through the city's Young Men's Christian Association. The Great Chicago Fire of 1871, the fire lesson 5.1 described, burned his church and his house together. Two years later he sailed for Britain with the singer Ira Sankey, and the two of them filled halls for two years. He came home famous.

What Moody did with that fame is the interesting part. He borrowed the city's own tools and turned them to revival. His campaigns ran on advance committees, rented halls, ticketed seating, printed hymnals, and choirs of hundreds. He preached in plain speech, avoided quarrels over doctrine, and aimed squarely at working people rather than at settled rural congregations. In 1886 he founded the Chicago training school that now carries his name, so that others could do the same work after him. The industrial city, in his hands, became a mission field rather than a lost cause.

A great deal of that energy went into direct relief. Jane Addamsjayn AD-umzA reformer who co-founded Hull House, a Chicago settlement house, and later became a leading pacifist. and Ellen Gates Starr opened Hull Househul howssA Chicago settlement house founded by Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr in 1889, offering education, childcare, and legal aid to immigrant neighbors. on Halsted Street in Chicago in 1889. The ward around it was crowded with Italian, Greek, Russian, and Bohemian families. The house offered a kindergarten, a boys' club, English classes, legal aid, and a public bath. It also offered a place to sit that was neither a saloon nor a tenement room. Its reformers lived in the building, and that was the point of the settlement idea. You resided among the people you served rather than visiting from a distance and going home at night.

Living there meant seeing things a visitor would miss, and the residents turned what they saw into evidence. Florence KelleyFLOR-enss KEL-eeA Hull House resident who documented conditions behind an 1893 Illinois factory law and became the state's chief factory inspector. lived at Hull House. She gathered the documentation behind an Illinois factory law in 1893, then became the state's chief factory inspector. In 1895 the residents published maps of their own neighborhood, recording wages and nationalities house by house. This was reform built on counted facts rather than on impressions. It points ahead to Week 12's photographs of tenement life and to the Progressive laws of Weeks 12 and 13. By 1910 more than four hundred settlement housesSET-ul-ment howssA reform institution where middle-class workers lived among the urban poor they served, offering services and gathering evidence for reform. were operating in American cities.

It is worth asking what settlement work was finally for. Addams had little patience with charity that made the giver feel generous and left the receiver exactly where he was. A bath and a kindergarten are genuinely useful. But the residents came to argue that the real trouble lay elsewhere: in wages, in hours, in housing law, and in the length of a child's working day. No amount of private kindness would reach any of those. That conclusion is what turned a house on Halsted Street into a source of legislation.

Other energy went abroad, and it went in remarkable volume. At a summer conference Moody hosted in 1886, a hundred college students pledged themselves to foreign missionary service. The movement that grew from that meeting took a watchword: the evangelization of the world in this generation. Under leaders like John R. Mott, thousands of students signed the pledge over the following decades. Denominational women's missionary societies raised their own funds and ran their own boards. They sent out their own single women as teachers, nurses, and doctors. Before women could vote anywhere in the country, these societies were the largest organized women's movement in it.

The work overseas deserves an honest accounting rather than a verdict. American missionaries built schools and hospitals in China, India, Africa, and the Ottoman Empire. They taught literacy and medicine that people genuinely wanted and often came a long way to get. They also carried a confidence that American Protestant civilization was the standard other peoples ought to meet. They were not always able to tell those two things apart. Both statements are true at once. Judge the enterprise by what it actually accomplished and cost, in particular places, rather than by its intentions alone.

There is one more thing to notice about where this energy went, and it matters for the next lesson. It flowed into Northern cities and across oceans more freely than it flowed into the rural South, where the large majority of Black Americans still lived. Many Northern settlement houses drew a color line of their own. The institution that carried religious and civic life for Black Southerners was therefore the one they had built themselves. The African Methodist Episcopal churches of Week 1 kept growing. In 1895 Black Baptists organized a national convention, which soon became the largest Black organization in the country. Its congregations ran schools, burial societies, and mutual aid funds where no state government would. Understand what a congregation was in such a county. It was often the only body Black families owned outright, chose their own officers for, and could assemble in without asking a white man's permission. That made it a school, a bank, a court of a kind, and a training ground in public speech, all at once. The next lesson takes up what those congregations were up against.

What tied urban revivalism, settlement work, and foreign missions together was a shared conviction that Christian faith obligated believers to act, and not merely to believe privately. That conviction found its most systematic statement in the Social Gospel movement, the subject of this week's second primary source. Its argument was that Christianity's obligations reached the economic structures of industrial society itself, not only individual souls. Whether an organized church could actually meet an obligation that large is a question this week will not settle for you.
